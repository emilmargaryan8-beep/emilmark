The Woman Who Refused to Die on a Tuesday

Emil Margaryan

🇫🇷 Français🇬🇧 English

English literary translation of the French original

Anoush Manoukian announced that she would die on a Tuesday. Wednesday did not suit her.

She was eighty-three, had two knees that predicted rain better than television, and three children who no longer called one another except when they needed to talk about her.

That day, all three of them were in her kitchen.

Aram had come from Yerevan an hour late with medicines Anoush already had. Mariam had brought vine leaves and set them on the table as though she had picked them herself, though Anoush recognised perfectly well the tins from the supermarket in Sevan. Vahan was on the phone by the window.

Anoush waited for him to hang up.

“I’m going to die on a Tuesday.”

Vahan looked at his phone.

“Today?”

“No.”

“Good. Then why are you telling us today?”

“So you can organise yourselves.”

Aram opened the cupboard where their mother kept her prescriptions.

“What did the doctor say?”

“That he’s a doctor.”

“Mum. Your heart.”

“He said it was tired.”

Mariam turned around.

“Your heart?”

“The doctor.”

The silence lasted just long enough to become serious.

Anoush poured the coffee.

She had lived alone since her husband died fifteen years earlier, in a house at the end of a street the residents still called School Street, although the school had become a potato warehouse. From her window, she could see the cemetery on the hill.

Her husband had been buried there on a Friday.

Anoush did not like Fridays either.

“Why Tuesday?” Mariam asked.

Anoush set a chipped cup in front of her.

“Because I know.”

“But how can you know something like that?”

“If I knew how, I’d know more.”

Vahan sighed.

He was already checking the time, as if the road were waiting for him.

They left before nightfall.

The following Tuesday, Mariam came back.

She walked in without knocking and found her mother in the garden pulling weeds.

“What are you doing?”

Anoush looked up.

“Dying.”

Mariam stood over her.

“Mum.”

“What?”

“You frighten us with these stories.”

Anoush straightened with difficulty.

“Then help me with the tomatoes. You can be frightened afterwards.”

Mariam helped her.

Around noon, Aram arrived with fish. Vahan called to say he would not be able to come.

At five, he came anyway.

No one spoke about death.

The Tuesday after that, there were three of them again.

Then four, because Mariam’s daughter came with her.

The following Tuesday, Aram brought his wife.

After two months, someone had the idea of bringing cake.

Later, no one could remember who had started it.

* * *

Anoush did not die that spring.

She did not die during the summer either.

The Tuesdays continued.

Some were noisy. Others so short you might have thought they had come only because they were afraid of missing something.

There was one Tuesday when Vahan and Aram argued over a piece of land their father had left behind the house.

“It’s mine,” Vahan said.

“Show me where that’s written.”

“Dad told me.”

“Dad said a lot of things.”

“He told me.”

Aram set down his glass.

“Of course he did.”

Anoush was peeling potatoes.

“Mum, did Dad give him the land?”

She kept peeling.

“Mum.”

“Your father is dead.”

“I know.”

“Then ask him.”

Vahan snorted. Aram picked up his glass again.

The following Tuesday, Vahan brought cucumbers. Without a word, he put a bag of them down in front of Aram.

They never spoke about the land again.

There was a Tuesday when three o’clock arrived before anyone else did.

Anoush ate alone.

She called no one.

At twenty past three, Mariam came in out of breath.

“I had a dentist’s appointment. I tried to call you.”

“I was here.”

“I know you were here.”

“Then why call?”

Mariam set her bag down a little too hard.

“Because normal people call.”

Anoush shrugged.

Aram arrived at four. Vahan at half past five.

Anoush had already cleared the table.

They ate whatever they could find.

* * *

In October, Aram fitted a grab handle in the bathroom without asking permission.

In November, Mariam brought a blanket Anoush found too heavy.

In December, Vahan changed the bulb in the hallway, which had been burnt out for two years.

“I could see perfectly well without it,” Anoush said.

“You kept bumping into the chest of drawers.”

“I knew where it was.”

“That’s exactly the problem.”

At Christmas, they came on a Sunday.

It felt strange.

They ate too much, spoke too loudly, handed out presents and left early.

Two days later, they came back.

It was Tuesday.

This time, no one needed a present.

* * *

The following year, Anoush’s granddaughter Lilit arrived with a young man.

His name was Tigran.

Anoush watched him eat for twenty minutes before asking:

“Do you want to marry her?”

Lilit turned red.

“Grandma!”

“I’m asking him.”

Tigran put down his fork.

“I don’t know yet.”

Anoush nodded.

“Good.”

“Good?” Lilit said.

“At least he thinks.”

Lilit protested.

But three Tuesdays later, Tigran came back.

Then he came again.

By spring, he knew where the plates were.

By autumn, he was repairing the gate without asking where the tools were kept.

Two years later, he sat beside Lilit with one hand resting on her stomach.

Anoush looked at the hand.

Then the stomach.

Then Lilit.

“When?”

“April.”

“What day?”

“We don’t know.”

“Avoid Tuesday.”

They laughed.

So did Anoush.

* * *

As the years passed, the question of Tuesday wore out before Anoush did.

On Tuesdays, people came. In time, that became answer enough.

Every Tuesday morning, Anoush got up before eight.

She opened the shutters.

She put water on to boil.

Then she took a small calendar from a drawer, one a pharmacy in Abovyan had given her years ago.

At the end of the day, she drew a line through the date.

No one knew the calendar existed.

The finished years were stacked at the back of the drawer.

Hundreds of Tuesdays crossed out.

* * *

Lilit’s baby was born on a Thursday.

Anoush was very pleased.

They named him Davit.

On his first Tuesday at his great-grandmother’s, he slept through almost the entire meal.

“He looks like his father,” Anoush said.

Tigran looked up.

“How am I supposed to take that?”

“Start by taking him in your arms.”

At four, Davit knew that Tuesday belonged to Anoush.

He did not know why.

At six, he asked:

“Why do we always come on Tuesday?”

The adults kept eating.

Anoush handed him a piece of lavash.

“Because on Tuesday, you come.”

Davit thought about this.

“Yes, but why?”

“Eat.”

He ate.

* * *

The first one to be truly absent was Vahan.

He did not die.

He went to work in Krasnodar.

At first, he called during the meal.

They propped the phone against the salt pot, and Vahan ate with them from a kitchen they never quite saw in full.

“Turn the phone a little,” Anoush would say.

“Why?”

“I want to see where you live.”

“You can see.”

“I can see your nose.”

“That’s already part of where I live.”

One day, he did not call.

Nor the following Tuesday.

Mariam asked whether they should ring him. Aram replied that he knew the number.

No one did.

The week after that, Vahan called as though nothing had happened.

His chair, however, never missed a Tuesday.

* * *

In the meantime, Anoush had turned eighty-nine.

Then ninety.

At ninety-one, she began walking with a cane.

At ninety-two, she forgot the neighbour’s name, but could still remember word for word what Mariam had said to her during an argument in 1987.

At ninety-three, she fell in the kitchen on a Friday.

She spent two days in hospital.

The doctor explained that she had been lucky.

“You see,” Anoush said to Mariam. “Friday.”

Mariam smoothed the blanket over her legs.

“Yes, Mum. Friday.”

The following Tuesday, there were nine people around her bed.

Anoush looked at them.

“Go home.”

No one moved.

“I’m not going to die with this many people in the room.”

“You said Tuesday,” Aram replied.

“There’ll be others.”

Aram lowered his eyes. For the first time, the thought that there might not be others seemed more serious than the thought of death.

* * *

There were others.

But Anoush ate less, and the others began filling the silence with more food.

Sometimes she spoke while everyone else was talking and no one heard her. Then she repeated the sentence, or let it go.

Davit grew.

Lilit divorced Tigran.

Tigran kept coming on certain Tuesdays. At first he asked whether he could come in. Then he stopped asking.

One Tuesday, Lilit arrived with another man.

Anoush looked at him.

“No.”

“Grandma!”

“I didn’t say anything.”

The man came a second time.

Not a third.

Tigran, meanwhile, came back to fix a leak.

* * *

Then came the Tuesday in February.

It had snowed during the night. The cemetery on the hill had disappeared beneath the white.

Aram was the first to arrive.

He found his mother sitting by the window, already dressed, her hair combed.

She would not eat breakfast.

Aram touched the radiator, checked the window, then remained standing there with useless hands.

At eleven, Mariam arrived.

At noon, Lilit and Davit.

Vahan appeared on the phone from Krasnodar.

At one, Anoush asked them to open the window a little.

“It’s cold,” Mariam said.

“I know.”

“You’ll catch cold.”

Anoush looked at her.

Mariam opened the window.

“Have it your way.”

Anoush listened.

“What are you listening to?” Davit asked.

She waited a moment.

“Nothing.”

But she went on listening.

They ate without her.

Around three, Anoush closed her eyes.

Aram stood up.

Mariam motioned for him to stay seated.

Only Davit kept eating.

At ten past four, Anoush opened her eyes.

“What time is it?”

“Ten past four.”

“Still?”

No one understood.

She closed her eyes again.

At five, she was still breathing.

At six as well.

At eight, she asked for yoghurt.

Mariam began to cry in the kitchen. When she came back, she had washed her face.

Anoush ate three spoonfuls.

Then she asked:

“Is it still Tuesday?”

“Yes.”

Anoush looked at the clock.

No one left.

At ten to midnight, they were all around her.

Even Vahan, on the phone propped against the salt pot.

Anoush watched the clock.

The others watched Anoush.

At midnight, the clock struck twelve times.

Davit counted to twelve.

No one spoke.

Anoush waited a few more seconds.

Then she said:

“Ah.”

Mariam leaned towards her.

“What?”

Anoush looked at her children.

“I was wrong.”

Aram gave a small laugh.

No one joined him.

“About what?” Lilit asked.

“It won’t be Tuesday.”

From the phone, Vahan asked:

“Can you hear me?”

No one answered at once.

* * *

On Wednesday morning, Anoush was better.

On Thursday too.

Aram went back to Yerevan.

Mariam went home.

Lilit drove Davit to school.

Vahan went back to work.

On Friday, no one came.

On Saturday, Mariam called.

On Sunday, Aram did too.

Monday was very quiet. Too quiet for a day that had never mattered.

Anoush spent part of the afternoon sorting lentils by the window.

That evening, she opened the drawer.

She looked at the calendar.

The next day was Tuesday.

She closed the drawer.

* * *

On Tuesday morning, she woke at twenty past seven.

For a few seconds, she lay still.

Then she got up.

She put water on to boil.

She took out six cups.

She stood looking at them.

Then she put five back in the cupboard, one by one.

She made her coffee.

At eleven, no one had come.

Nor at noon.

The silence had taken back the whole house.

Anoush cut a tomato, some cheese and a piece of bread.

She ate slowly.

At twenty to one, she cleared away.

She washed her plate.

She wiped the table.

Then she went to her bedroom and changed the sheets.

Around two, a car slowed in front of the house, then drove on.

Anoush did not look. She had spent enough years waiting for people who eventually came in.

At twenty past two, someone opened the gate.

Mariam came in carrying a bag.

“There wasn’t any good bread left.”

Anoush kept folding the sheet.

“Then why did you buy any?”

“Because Aram eats anything if you let him.”

She put the bag in the kitchen.

A few minutes later, Aram came in.

“Were you behind me?” Mariam asked.

“No.”

“I saw you.”

“Then why are you asking?”

He put a bottle of water on the table.

“Where’s Mum?”

“In the bedroom.”

“Is she all right?”

Mariam looked at him.

“Go and see.”

At ten past three, Lilit arrived with Davit.

At half past three, the phone rang.

Vahan.

Mariam propped it against the salt pot.

No one had set the table.

So, without discussing it, they did.

Aram took out the plates.

Lilit cut the bread.

Davit looked for the glasses.

Anoush came into the kitchen.

She stopped in the doorway.

Mariam was scolding Aram for leaving the cheese out of the fridge.

Aram was saying one hour would not kill anyone.

Vahan was shouting from the phone that he could not hear a thing.

Davit had taken someone else’s chair.

Anoush sat down.

“Who’s hungry?” Mariam asked.

Everyone answered at once.

* * *

That evening, they left.

Mariam was the last to go.

She kissed her mother on the forehead.

“See you Tuesday.”

She froze.

So did Anoush.

Mariam opened her mouth as if to correct herself.

Then she settled her coat properly on her shoulder.

“Lock up properly after me.”

She left.

Anoush closed the door.

She put away two cups, left the others to dry and turned off the kitchen light.

Before going to bed, she opened the drawer.

She took out the calendar.

For years, every Tuesday that had passed bore a black line.

Some lines were straight.

Others trembled.

One Tuesday had been crossed out twice.

Anoush picked up the pencil.

She looked at the day’s date.

Then she put the pencil back in the drawer.

Tuesday remained untouched.
