The Laughter in the Void

Emil Margaryan

🇫🇷 Français🇬🇧 English🇷🇺 Русский

English literary version of the French original

Rain fell over Edinburgh with the peculiar patience it seemed to reserve for the city: it did not beat against windows or facades; it settled in. A light mist hung between the stone buildings, and the cobbles reflected the yellow glow of the streetlamps.

They had been walking for several minutes without speaking.

He kept his hands buried in the pockets of his coat and stared straight ahead. She followed a few paces behind, shoulders drawn in, watching where she stepped, as though one false movement might open something beneath their feet.

At the corner, he slowed and turned.

— Are you coming?

She lifted her head and nodded.

He waited for her to catch up.

Their building stood at the end of a narrow street, behind a black gate that creaked in the damp. They climbed the three flights without exchanging a word. Their footsteps kept different rhythms: his were heavy and regular; hers sometimes stopped for barely a second, as though she had forgotten why she was climbing.

The moment they entered the flat, she took off her coat and crossed the hallway.

He knew where she was going.

— You could wait a little.

She did not answer.

The door to the small bedroom stood ajar.

She pushed it open.

Nothing had moved.

The narrow bed against the wall. The wooden blocks beside the rug. Two little cars beneath a chair. On the shelf, books with worn corners, shelved crookedly. A brown bear with one ear drooping. And, hanging from a hook behind the door, the old blue coat.

She went over to it.

Her fingers brushed the sleeve.

— He loved this coat so much...

He remained in the doorway.

— Yes.

She turned to him.

— Do you remember the day he came home covered in mud?

He hesitated.

— Which day?

— At the park. He ran towards us and slipped right at my feet. He had mud in his hair.

A smile crossed her face.

— He was laughing so hard we couldn't even tell him off.

He looked at the coat.

— Yes... at the park.

She waited.

— You don't remember?

— I do. Of course I do.

— What did he say?

He raised his head.

— What?

— When he fell. You laughed because he said something.

He tried to summon the moment.

It hovered somewhere behind his eyes, but without edges. Wet grass. A child running. Perhaps a bench. Perhaps his wife's laughter.

— I can't place it.

She took the sleeve of the coat between her fingers again.

— He said, “The ground caught me.”

He smiled.

— Yes. That's right.

But he no longer knew whether the words had truly come back to him or she had simply put them there.

In the sitting room, she opened the sideboard drawer and took out the album.

She set it between them.

— Look.

The thick pages crackled beneath her fingers. Under the transparent sheets, some of the photographs had yellowed at the edges.

She stopped.

— His second birthday.

He leaned closer.

In the photograph, the child sat in front of a cake. A red toy lorry filled almost the entire bottom of the picture.

— We bought him that, she said.

— He wouldn't blow out the candles.

This time he had answered without hesitation.

She looked up at him.

— Yes.

— He was playing with the wrapping paper.

— See? You do remember.

He barely smiled.

— Of course.

She turned the page.

— And this one, at my parents' house.

He studied the photograph for longer.

A checked tablecloth. A lawn. Two figures in the background. The child, turned towards something outside the frame.

— He was laughing, she said.

— He was always laughing.

She ran a finger over the photograph.

— No. Not always.

He said nothing.

* * *

That evening they ate almost without speaking.

The clink of cutlery sounded too sharp in the kitchen. Several times she glanced towards the hallway.

— Sometimes I wonder how other people do it.

— What other people?

— The ones this happens to.

He put down his fork.

She was staring at her plate.

— How they go on afterwards.

He took his time before answering.

— We go on because the days do.

— That's not an answer.

— I don't have another one.

She pushed her plate away.

— I don't feel as though we're going on. I feel as though we're only passing through the days.

He meant to reach for her hand, but she stood before he could.

Later they sat side by side on the sofa without turning on the television.

— I often think about what he would have become, she said.

He stared at the window.

— So do I.

— How do you picture him?

The question caught him off guard.

— Taller.

She gave a sad smile.

— Well, obviously.

— I don't know. Maybe... as tall as me.

— He would have had your hair.

He ran a hand over his head without thinking.

She watched the gesture. A shadow crossed her face.

— Do you remember?

He turned to her.

— What?

— When he used to say he wanted your hair.

He let the question settle.

Then a scene surfaced—or something like one.

The boy was sitting between them on the sofa, wiggling his fingers above his head to imitate his father's unruly hair.

— Mum, why is Dad's hair always such a mess?

She was laughing.

— Because your father is always in too much of a hurry in the morning to comb it.

He put on a look of mock offence.

— I prefer the natural look. Your mum's the one who spends half the morning doing her hair.

The boy had looked down at the sofa.

— Is that why there's always hair everywhere?

All three of them had laughed.

The boy had sat up straighter, pleased with himself.

— I want hair like Dad's. Messy.

She had stroked his head.

— When you're grown up, you can do whatever you like.

Then he had nestled against her.

— When I'm grown up, I'll stay with you. The three of us, always.

She had answered:

— Always.

The scene ended there.

He sat very still.

— Yes, he said at last. I can see it.

One detail troubled him.

In that scene, he could not hear the child's voice.

Only his laughter.

* * *

That night she barely slept.

At around three, he woke to find the other side of the bed empty. He got up.

She was sitting on the floor of the small bedroom, the brown bear in her lap.

— You should come back to bed.

She did not move.

— He slept with it.

— Yes.

— Always.

He crouched beside her.

— I thought it was the blue dog.

She slowly raised her head.

— What blue dog?

— The soft toy. The little dog.

— He never had a blue dog.

He frowned.

Yet he could see it: the limp body, the ears too long, the blue fabric faded by washing.

— He did.

— No.

— I'm almost certain.

— It was the bear.

He looked at the toy in her lap.

— Maybe.

She hugged the bear to herself.

— Why are you doing this?

— I'm not doing anything.

— Lately, every time I talk about him, you hesitate.

— I'm trying to get the details straight.

— You shouldn't have to try.

He stood.

The album had been left open on the desk.

She picked it up.

— Look at this one.

A photograph showed the child in front of a table decorated with garlands.

— He was four. My sister's birthday.

He studied the picture.

— Are you sure?

She stiffened.

— About what?

— That he was four.

— Of course.

— I thought...

He stopped.

— What?

— Nothing.

— Say it.

— I thought he was still three when your sister moved to Aberdeen.

His wife's face closed.

— She moved afterwards.

— Are you sure?

She shut the album.

— Why do you keep asking me if I'm sure?

— Because I don't know any more.

— I do.

— Fine.

— You want to erase him.

— What?

— You're erasing him piece by piece.

— Stop it.

— The park. The dog. Now the birthday.

— I'm not erasing anything!

His voice rang through the room.

They fell silent.

Then she asked very softly:

— Did you love him?

He looked at her.

For a moment, he could not answer.

— Of course I loved him.

She lowered her eyes.

In the silence that followed, they heard a noise.

Very faint.

He turned his head.

— Did you hear that?

She was already looking at him.

— Hear what?

He waited.

Nothing more.

— Nothing.

* * *

The next day, she wanted to go to the park.

The trees were shedding their leaves and the paths were almost deserted. She walked to a bench near the swings.

— It was here.

He remained standing.

— What was?

She stared at him.

— The mud.

— Oh.

She looked at the swings.

— He came running from there.

She pointed towards the path.

— Then he slipped right in front of you.

He looked at the ground.

— In front of me?

— Yes.

— Yesterday you said he fell at your feet.

She was silent.

— It's the same thing.

— No.

— Why are you doing this?

— I'm only trying to understand.

She stood.

— I want to go home.

Neither of them spoke on the way back.

That afternoon, she began talking about the night he had splashed half the bathwater onto the floor.

She could still see the steam on the mirror, the water on the tiles, the splashes on her blouse. She could almost feel the small hands pushing hers away as she tried to wash his hair.

She could even hear herself laughing.

— You're going to empty the whole bath!

The boy laughed harder.

Her husband listened to her story.

Then he said:

— That night, you were in bed.

She stopped.

— What?

— You had a fever.

— No.

— You did. I gave him his bath.

She shook her head.

— I can see the water.

— So do I.

— And his skin. His wet hair. The smell of the soap.

— So do I.

They looked at each other.

This time neither tried to persuade the other.

* * *

Night fell early.

She stood at the window for a long time. The rain had started again.

Then a smile caught at her mouth.

— What? he asked.

— Nothing.

— You're smiling.

— I was thinking about him.

He waited.

— About what?

— His laugh.

She closed her eyes.

— Sometimes I feel as though I can hear it.

His chest tightened.

— So do I.

She opened her eyes.

— Since when?

He did not know how to answer.

A sound drifted along the hallway.

Brief.

Almost a breath.

She stood.

— Did you hear that?

This time he nodded.

They remained motionless.

Then she walked towards the bedroom.

— Wait.

She ignored him.

She put her hand on the handle.

The door opened.

She did not scream.

Her silence frightened him more than a scream would have.

She simply stood there.

He came up behind her.

The room was empty.

Not like a room someone had cleared out.

Empty in some other way.

The walls were bare. The bed, perfectly made, did not look like a child's bed. There was no shelf. No blocks. No cars. No books.

Behind the door, the hook was gone.

She stepped inside.

She reached towards the space where the shelf had been.

— No.

He moved past her.

— The coat...

He touched the wall.

There was no mark to show that a hook had ever been fixed there.

— It was here.

— Yes.

— I saw it.

— So did I.

She rushed into the sitting room.

The sideboard drawer stuck, then came free.

The album was there.

She seized it and opened it.

On the first page: the two of them in front of a house.

Then a Christmas: her and him.

On the next page the picnic at her parents' house appeared again: the checked tablecloth, the lawn, the two figures in the background.

She brought the photograph close to her face.

— No.

— What?

She handed him the album.

At the centre of the picture, between them, there was no one.

Only an empty chair.

His legs weakened.

— It wasn't like this.

She was already turning the pages.

The birthday cake.

Two adults.

A red toy lorry in front of an empty chair.

A muffled sound escaped her.

— He was there.

— Yes.

— You could see him.

— Yes.

— Say it.

— I could see him.

She went on.

Photograph after photograph, there were only two of them.

Always two.

And yet, in places, something remained.

A child's plate.

A pair of little boots at the edge of a photograph.

The red lorry.

A shadow whose shape neither of them recognised.

She set the album down.

— His papers.

He looked at her.

— What papers?

— His papers. His birth certificate. His medical records. There has to be something.

They searched.

The desk. The drawers. A metal box containing bills, their marriage certificate, letters, receipts.

At the bottom of the box, beneath a bundle of bills, she found a cassette tape.

For a moment she did not move.

On the label, written in blue felt-tip pen, was a single word, almost faded away: “Christmas”.

— That's him.

He took the cassette.

— Are you sure?

— We recorded him at my parents' house. It was his last Christmas there.

The recording meant nothing to him.

They found the old tape recorder in the sitting-room cabinet. She inserted the cassette, rewound it and pressed play.

At first there was only hiss.

Then came distant voices, warped by age. Glasses being set on a table. A door. Someone speaking too far from the microphone.

And a laugh.

She grabbed his arm.

— Do you hear it?

He leaned towards the machine.

The laugh came again, followed by a few indistinct words.

— That's him, she whispered.

He closed his eyes.

For a few seconds he wanted to believe her. There was something familiar in the sound: the way the speaker caught his breath, then let the last syllable fall away, as though the laughter continued somewhere at the back of his throat.

The tape rolled on.

A sentence suddenly came through more clearly.

He opened his eyes.

— Go back.

— What?

— Go back to that bit.

She rewound a few seconds.

The laugh. The breath. Then the sentence.

His hands went cold.

— Again.

She played it again.

This time he stopped listening for the child he thought he had heard and listened instead to the voice itself.

He knew it.

— What is it? she asked.

He did not answer.

The way certain words were swallowed. The laugh that broke in the middle. The short breath before speaking.

It was his voice.

Not his voice now—younger, higher. But unmistakably his.

He knew it from the old childhood tapes his mother had kept.

He turned the cassette over in his hands.

The handwriting on the label looked like his wife's.

Or perhaps he merely wanted it to.

— Well? she asked. Can you hear him?

He looked up at her.

She was waiting for his answer with such fragile certainty that he could not bring himself to break it.

— Yes, he said.

She pressed play.

His own childhood voice laughed again from the tape.

Then the tape went silent.

She searched the box again.

— There has to be something.

— Maybe somewhere else.

— Where?

— I don't know.

She emptied the box onto the floor.

— Stop.

— No.

— Stop.

She looked up at him.

— Do you think he never existed?

He did not answer.

— Say it.

— I don't know what I think.

— You remember him.

— Yes.

— Then he existed.

He was about to answer.

But a question came to him.

— What was his middle name?

She opened her mouth.

No sound came out.

He immediately regretted asking.

— I...

She pressed her hands to her temples.

— Wait.

— It doesn't matter.

— It does.

— No.

— I know it.

She closed her eyes.

— I know it.

He waited.

The silence stretched.

— And his birthday? he asked despite himself.

She opened her eyes.

— You've just seen the photographs.

— The date.

She went pale.

He searched too.

He knew the cake, the red lorry, the wrapping paper. He could almost smell the candle wax.

But no date came back to him.

— My God, she whispered.

He sat down.

Outside, a car passed. The sound of its tyres on the wet cobbles slowly faded.

She remained standing among the papers.

— I carried him inside me.

He raised his head.

— I remember carrying him.

He said nothing.

— I remember his weight.

She placed a hand on her stomach.

— I remember the first time he moved.

Her voice broke.

— I remember.

He stood and went towards her.

— I believe you.

She stepped back.

— Don't believe me. Think back.

He stopped.

She was crying now, without a sound.

— Find him for yourself.

He tried.

The birth.

A hospital.

A room.

The first time he had held his son in his arms.

All he found was a white light.

A window.

His wife lying down.

Then nothing.

No face.

No weight.

No first cry.

He closed his eyes.

And then he heard the laughter.

Clear.

Very close.

He opened his eyes.

She had stopped crying.

— Did you hear that?

He did not answer.

The laughter came again.

A child's laugh, brief and light.

It came from the hallway.

She went first.

They reached the empty bedroom.

The door was still open.

Nothing moved inside.

— That's him, she whispered.

He stared into the room.

— Yes.

She turned to him.

— Did you recognise him?

He was about to say yes.

The word reached his lips.

But something stopped him.

The laugh he had just heard matched neither the one in his head nor the one on the tape.

In his mind, the boy's laugh was deeper, almost hoarse when it went on too long.

This one was brighter.

Higher.

He looked at his wife.

She was smiling through her tears.

— That's him, she said again.

From her smile, he understood that perhaps she had not heard the same laugh he had.

He said nothing.

They remained outside the bedroom.

She went into the empty room.

He stayed on the threshold.

— Do you remember what he used to say? she asked.

— When?

— On the sofa. When he talked about the three of us.

His gaze settled on the bare wall.

— Yes.

— What did he say?

He tried to answer.

The three of us, always.

But suddenly he was no longer certain the child had ever said those words.

Perhaps she had said them.

Perhaps he had.

Perhaps no one had.

He looked at his wife.

She waited.

Then, in the room that no longer held a single trace of him, the laugh came a third time.

This time neither of them said:

“That's him.”
