The Three Hundred and First Inhabitant

Emil Margaryan

🇫🇷 Français🇬🇧 English🇷🇺 Русский

English literary translation of the French original

The bus dropped Arpi Vardanyan in front of a sign on which the village name had lost three letters.

Only DZOR—HEN remained. The wind took care of the rest.

“Town hall?” she asked the driver.

“The house with the flag.”

He thought for a moment.

“Or the one with the dog. The flag changes houses when it rains.”

Arpi got off with her suitcase, her tablet and an official assignment from the Statistics Committee. She was thirty-one and believed in numbers because they were the only things that did not change their story when questioned separately.

In five years of service, she had encountered dead people who still voted, living people born under two different spellings, and a shepherd registered as single in three municipalities. She had put them back into their proper boxes without anger. Once corrected, an error seemed almost grateful to her.

Yet, folded at the bottom of her bag, she still kept the complaint of an old woman. During her first inspection, Arpi had deleted a duplicate record and, with it, the last administrative reason for a nurse to climb up to the woman’s hamlet every week. The number had become correct. The old woman, meanwhile, had had to come down.

Dzorashen was the first village where an error seemed to be waiting for her.

The mayor was waiting beneath a mulberry tree. He wore a brown suit, dust-covered shoes and a tie that had given up trying to tighten anything.

“Hovsep Bedrossian,” he said. “Mayor of Dzorashen for seventeen years and provisionally since forever.”

“Arpi Vardanyan. Population inspection.”

“We were already inspected in April.”

“You had three hundred and one inhabitants in April.”

“We still have three hundred and one inhabitants.”

“I counted three hundred in the updated database.”

Hovsep looked around, as though the missing person might be standing behind the tree.

“Then you updated it badly.”

The new regulation required a recent photograph for anyone whose civil record had never been digitised. Dzorashen lacked exactly one photograph: that of Hagop Hagopian, born on February 7, 1944, residing at No. 6, Spring Street.

Without him, the village fell below the threshold set by the regional decree. At three hundred and one, it kept an independent school, a clinic open two days a week and a daily bus. At three hundred, the school became an annex, the doctor became a telephone number, and the bus became a possibility to be considered at a later date.

“Where is Hagop Hagopian?” Arpi asked.

“Here.”

“Here where?”

“You begin with the difficult questions.”

Hovsep picked up her suitcase. He led her to the town hall, a white room where four official portraits had succeeded one another without anyone filling in the old nail holes. On the desk lay two registers, a telephone and a plate of mulberries.

A boy of about twelve was sitting in the mayor’s chair.

“This is Narek,” Hovsep said. “My grandson. He doesn’t work here.”

“I’m in charge of the internet,” Narek clarified.

“We don’t have internet.”

“That’s why I don’t work.”

Arpi turned on her tablet.

“I need to see Mr. Hagopian, verify his identity and take his photograph.”

“Today?” Hovsep asked.

“Preferably before his next birth.”

Narek laughed. Hovsep picked up a mulberry and examined it for a long time.

“Hagop does not like being photographed.”

“No one does. The state does it anyway.”

“That is why he prefers the village.”

Hagop’s register ran to twelve pages. He had been born once, married three times, divorced four, completed his military service in two units and received a medal for a battle in which neither regiment had taken part. His height ranged from 1.59 to 1.82 metres. His eyes had successively been grey, black and “ordinary.” Under “blood group” appeared A positive, O negative and a coffee stain.

“He changed a great deal,” Hovsep said.

“He changed blood?”

“After his second marriage, everyone thought he was different.”

Arpi looked up. In the margin, someone had written: DECEASED, 1991. Another pen had crossed out the word and added: 1993, WRONG PERSON.

“He died in 1991?”

“A bad year.”

“And then it turned out to be a mistake?”

“A better year.”

“He received his pension until last month.”

“You see? He recovered.”

Arpi called her supervisor in Yerevan. Lilit answered while chewing something dry.

“Is the person concerned alive?” she asked.

“That is precisely what I am trying to establish.”

“If he can sign, he is alive.”

“He has eleven different signatures.”

“Use the most recent one.”

“It may belong to someone else.”

“Then use the most legible. We don’t do handwriting analysis.”

Arpi lowered her voice.

“I suspect a collective identity.”

A silence crossed the hundred and twenty kilometres between them.

“How many people?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then it is not collective yet. Find the person first.”

“And if the person remains untraceable?”

“His file stays active until the next inspection. One year. After that, there will have to be a face or a death.”

Lilit reminded her that a fraud report would freeze the pension, trigger an audit of municipal accounts and probably lead to the immediate suspension of services calculated according to population.

“You wanted an exact number,” she concluded. “Try not to find too many.”

Arpi closed the register.

“Show me his house.”

Spring Street no longer had a spring. It had six houses, which spared it the problem of numbering. The last one was blue and small, with a grapevine entering through one window and leaving through another.

Hovsep opened the door with a key taken from his own pocket.

Inside stood a narrow bed, seven jackets hanging from the same nail and a row of shoes, none of which made a pair. On a table, someone had left fresh bread, a box of pills and a letter addressed to Glendale, California.

“He lives alone?” Arpi asked.

“Depends on the day.”

“Where does he sleep?”

“When he sleeps here, in the bed.”

“And when he doesn’t sleep here?”

“You see? You already know.”

Arpi opened the wardrobe. She found a Soviet uniform, a wedding suit, two crutches, a violin without strings and a child’s schoolbag.

“Whose things are these?”

“Hagop’s.”

“All of them?”

“Not at the same time.”

An old woman appeared in the doorway. She wore a purple headscarf and carried a bowl of beans.

“You are searching the house of an absent man,” she said.

“I’m looking for him.”

“Then don’t search. People rarely hide in their wardrobes after eighty.”

Hovsep introduced Siranouch, the former town clerk. She was eighty-nine, possessed an exact memory for other people’s mistakes and a deafness she used only against useless questions.

“Do you know Hagop Hagopian?” Arpi asked.

“Better than he did.”

“Where is he?”

“Which one?”

Hovsep closed his eyes.

Siranouch set the beans on the table.

“The first Hagop was born in 1944,” she said. “That one your register did not invent.”

He was the son of a widow, a skinny boy who refused apricots and slept with a piece of string tied around his wrist so he would not lose his dreams. At seven, he fell into the irrigation canal. They found one sandal near the bridge and nothing else.

His mother did not report his death.

“She hoped he would come back?” Arpi asked.

“She had a sick baby, and Hagop’s ration card entitled her to milk,” Siranouch replied. “Hope is a beautiful thing. Milk was more urgent.”

For a few months, the absent boy therefore continued to eat. Then a fatherless teenager needed a certificate to enter technical school. They lent him the birth certificate. Later, a man returning from prison used the name to find work. A conscript carried it into the army. A woman married it in order to give her child an official father.

“She married a dead man?”

“She had already married a living one. It had gone less well.”

Hagop became the name the village took out whenever the law demanded a person and life had failed to prepare a suitable one.

“This is organised fraud,” Arpi said.

“No,” Siranouch replied. “If it had been organised, it would have been neater.”

Arpi pointed to the contradictory pages.

“Who filled out these documents?”

“I did, some of them.”

“And the others?”

“People who were more dishonest. They had better handwriting.”

“You could be prosecuted.”

“At my age, anything that runs gets tired before I do.”

Hovsep intervened.

“Hagop never made anyone rich.”

“He receives a pension.”

“It pays for the school heating.”

“That makes it embezzlement.”

Narek, who had followed them, raised his hand as though in class.

“If Hagop pays for our heating, can we say he attends the school?”

“No.”

“Then someone pays without learning anything. Isn’t the school stealing from him?”

He led Arpi to the school. The building had two classrooms, eleven pupils, seventeen desks and a stove fed with logs that were too long. A teacher split the largest ones with an axe while the children conjugated the verb to leave.

“Who is Hagop Hagopian?” Arpi asked.

The children pointed at the stove.

“He pays for the wood,” the teacher explained.

“You know he may not be a real person?”

A little girl in the front row raised her hand.

“The heating isn’t always real either.”

“That’s not the question.”

“When it is real, it’s because of Hagop.”

On the wall, among the drawings, Arpi noticed eleven portraits of the same man. In one he had a green beard, in another six fingers, and in Narek’s drawing no face at all. Only a black suit filled with tiny names.

“Why did you draw him like that?”

“I didn’t know which one to choose.”

“You’ve never seen him.”

“Neither have you. Yet you came for him.”

During her training, Arpi had been taught that a census counted people, not the reasons why they needed to be counted. She had copied the sentence into a notebook. She wondered where she had put it.

Arpi asked to see the villagers who had used the name. They gathered in the community hall, which had never known a celebration large enough to justify its title.

Nine came. Seven agreed to speak. The others had come to make sure no one spoke on their behalf.

Garnik, eighty-two, had completed his military service with Hagop’s papers because his own gave the army the age of his older brother.

“You could be photographed,” Hovsep said. “You are about the right age.”

“Impossible,” Garnik replied. “My wife thinks I’m Garnik.”

“For fifty-six years?”

“She is only beginning to get used to it.”

Anahid had been married to Hagop for eleven months. When Arpi asked to whom, she pointed at a man by the window.

“My name is Rouben,” the man protested.

“Now, yes. When you drank, you were Hagop.”

A third man had spent six months in prison under the name so his mother would not learn of his arrest.

“She never found out?”

“She came to see me every Sunday.”

“Then why change your name?”

The man smoothed his sleeves before answering.

“My mother could tolerate prison. Not the neighbours.”

Levon, the baker, had carried the name for one winter after returning from Russia. His birth certificate had disappeared between two consulates and the gas company refused to connect a bakery belonging to a man whom the country did not recognise. Hagop signed the contract. Levon baked the bread.

“Is the bread in his house from you?” Arpi asked.

“Every Monday.”

“You are still repaying a debt twenty years old?”

“At first, yes. Now I simply dislike the idea that he might come home and find the table empty.”

Hasmik, a former nurse, placed a photograph of a swaddled infant in front of Arpi. Thirty years earlier, the child had been abandoned at the clinic during a snowstorm. No form allowed them to keep it without a declared parent. For one night, Hagop had therefore been its father.

“Where is that child now?”

“In Gyumri. He is well.”

“Does he know this story?”

Hasmik took back the photograph.

“He knows what is useful to him.”

Arpi laid some of the objects from the house on the table. Garnik recognised the uniform still hanging at No. 6. Rouben took back the wedding suit without asking Anahid’s permission. The crutches belonged to a man who had died under his own name, which seemed to reassure everyone. No one claimed the violin.

“And the shoes?” Arpi asked.

Several villagers lowered their eyes.

“Hagop sometimes worked nights,” Hovsep said.

“With six feet?”

“That may explain his career.”

A woman sitting near the door then said that her son had used Hagop’s passport to leave the country thirty-two years earlier. Since then, he had lived in Lyon under another name.

“Which one?” Arpi asked.

“If he had told me which one, I could write to him.”

The laughter stopped. The woman by the door kept her hands folded over her bag. Arpi closed the register.

“And yet I need a face,” Arpi said.

“Use Garnik’s,” Hovsep suggested.

“My wife will recognise me.”

“Rouben’s, then.”

“I already gave eleven months,” Rouben said.

Siranouch watched the meeting from the back row.

“Photograph the house,” she said.

“The software requires a human face.”

“That is a recent development in software?”

Arpi left the room before she laughed. A civil servant was not supposed to laugh in the middle of an irregularity; it could be interpreted as approval.

She returned alone to No. 6.

The light was fading. Villagers entered one after another without asking permission. A woman took the medicine. An old man carried off the bread without cutting it. Another removed the uniform from the wall. A young woman added a letter to those lying on the table.

“Do you live here?” Arpi asked her.

“No. My brother is in Krasnodar. The letters are sent from here.”

“Why?”

“He only replies to Hagop.”

When everyone had gone, the house seemed almost empty. Arpi understood that the objects were not being kept there. They were waiting for their owner, or for someone close enough to be mistaken for him.

Inside the schoolbag, she found a notebook covered in Siranouch’s handwriting. Each page bore a name, a date and a reason.

Garnik — eighteen months — army.

Anahid and Rouben — eleven months — child.

Sarkis — six months — prison.

On the last page, a recent line read: Dzorashen School — seven years — heating.

Below it, Narek had added in pencil: Hagop Hagopian — eighty years — village.

Siranouch was standing behind her.

“He wrote eighty because he subtracted the two years Hagop was dead,” she said.

“Why did you keep this notebook?”

“So the lies would not mistake themselves for the truth.”

“And yet you fabricated an entire life.”

“No. A life fits inside a single body. We created someone several bodies could carry when their own were no longer enough.”

Arpi thought of the report she had to send the next day. The facts would not fit neatly inside it.

“The first Hagop is dead,” she said.

“Probably.”

“All the others exist under their own names.”

“On good days.”

“Then Hagop no longer exists.”

Siranouch looked at the empty table.

“You are here because of him. The doctor comes because of him. The bus will return tomorrow because of him. For someone who does not exist, he gives you a great deal of work.”

At 11:58 p.m., the tablet reminded Arpi that she had twelve hours left to conclude the inspection. She photographed every page of the notebook, then prepared two reports. In the first, Hagop was an old fraud maintained by the municipality. In the second, he was an untraceable person whose situation required further verification.

The first told the truth more plainly. The second kept the school open.

She thought of the son in Lyon, living under a name his mother did not know. The same Hagop who kept eleven children warm had left one woman without an address to write to.

Arpi held her finger over the SEND command. With her other hand, she checked mechanically that the folded piece of paper was still in her bag.

She sent neither report.

Shortly after midnight, the tablet announced that the network had returned. Either report could now be sent with a single tap. Arpi switched off the connection.

Footsteps passed outside the door. When she opened it, no one was there. On the threshold lay a bundle of dry wood and a loaf of bread still warm. At the end of the street, a figure turned behind the old fountain.

“Hagop?” she called.

The man did not turn around. Arpi did not call a second time.

She brought the wood inside. She placed the bread on the table, in exactly the same place as the first loaf.

She slept in Hagop’s bed. Beneath the pillow, she found the second sandal of the vanished boy. The leather was hard, almost black. Inside, someone had written in pencil: IN CASE HE COMES BACK WITH ONLY ONE FOOT.

At first Arpi thought the sentence was funny. Then she understood that Hagop’s mother must have considered it serious. Between the two thoughts, she barely slept.

The next morning, the entire village was waiting outside the town hall. Hovsep had placed a chair against the white wall.

“We are ready for the photograph,” he announced.

“Who is going to sit down?”

“We have not decided yet.”

Garnik suggested Rouben. Rouben suggested the former prisoner. The former prisoner suggested Hovsep, on the grounds that a mayor was already paid to represent people he was not.

Narek placed the sandal on the chair.

“This is what remains of the first one.”

Arpi restored the connection and opened the application. The network still held. Four options appeared under the name Hagop Hagopian: PRESENT, TEMPORARILY ABSENT, UNTRACEABLE, DECEASED.

“Dead,” Garnik said.

“Present,” Hovsep said.

“Untraceable,” Rouben said.

“He cannot be all three,” Arpi said.

Siranouch shrugged.

“And yet that is what he has done best.”

Arpi selected UNTRACEABLE.

The application asked: LAST PERSON TO HAVE ATTESTED TO HIS EXISTENCE.

She looked at Hovsep.

“Do not look at me,” he said. “I am mayor. I only attest what has already been attested.”

She looked at Siranouch.

“My handwriting is in the notebook,” the old woman replied. “Yours will be in the computer.”

Arpi entered her own name.

The file was placed under deferred review. Dzorashen gained one year. No one applauded.

Before leaving, Narek asked Arpi to photograph the inhabitants in front of the town hall.

They lined up badly. The tall people hid the short ones, the living held portraits of the dead, and Garnik changed places three times to avoid standing next to his wife.

“How many are we?” Hovsep shouted.

Arpi counted the people in front of her.

“Three hundred.”

“One is missing,” Narek said.

“Hagop?”

“No. You.”

Narek stepped out of the group, placed the tablet on a windowsill and set the timer. Arpi joined the villagers. The boy ran to stand in front of her at the last second. Just before the photograph, she noticed the empty chair against the wall and, on it, the small sandal.

The bus honked at the end of the street.

Narek counted to three.

This time, no one knew exactly who had been photographed.
