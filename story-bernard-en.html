The Apologies of Mr. Bernard

Emil Margaryan

🇫🇷 Français🇬🇧 English

English literary translation of the French original

Bernard Lemaire decided to become a better man on a Thursday because his dentist had cancelled.

He was fifty-one.

At twenty past ten, he found himself at home with an entire morning he had not planned for and an anaesthetic he had not, in the end, received.

He made coffee.

He opened the window.

In the street, a truck was blocking a car from pulling out of its parking space. The owner of the car was honking with admirable regularity.

Bernard closed the window.

For three months, he had been reading a book about forgiveness.

He had bought it after his divorce.

The divorce had nothing to do with forgiveness, but bookshops often put the two subjects close together.

Bernard took a notebook.

On the first page, he wrote:

PEOPLE I OWE AN APOLOGY TO

He underlined it twice.

Then he waited.

The first name came easily.

1. Philippe Moreau.

At school, Bernard had borrowed a Pink Floyd record from Philippe and never given it back.

He hesitated.

Perhaps that was not enough.

He wrote it down anyway.

The second was more serious.

2. Sophie.

Sophie had been his wife for eleven years.

3. Mum.

He crossed out the name.

His mother had been dead for six years.

He wrote it again.

3. Mum.

After some thought, he added:

4. Monsieur Girard.

5. Claire Dumas.

6. Éric.

7. Nathalie.

He stopped.

Then he added two more names.

Underneath, he wrote:

10.

Nothing after it.

He put down his pen.

By twenty to twelve, he had nine people and one empty place.

That seemed enough to begin.

He closed the notebook.

* * *

Philippe Moreau still lived in Marseille.

Bernard found him online.

He sold kitchens in Aubagne.

They arranged to meet in a café near Castellane.

Philippe arrived fifteen minutes late and twenty kilos heavier than Bernard remembered him.

“Bernard?”

“Philippe.”

They shook hands.

Philippe sat down.

“How long has it been? Thirty years?”

“Thirty-three.”

“You counted?”

“No.”

Bernard had counted.

They ordered two coffees.

Philippe talked about his business, his two daughters, and a dispute with the co-owners of his building that had been going on since 2019.

Bernard waited for an opening.

None came.

He made one.

“Philippe, there’s something I wanted to tell you.”

Philippe put down his sugar.

“Go on.”

“At school, you lent me The Dark Side of the Moon.”

Philippe looked at him.

“Maybe.”

“I never gave it back.”

“Oh.”

“I wanted to apologise.”

Philippe was silent.

Bernard waited.

“All right,” Philippe said.

“It came back to me recently.”

“Fine.”

Philippe drank his coffee.

“I forgive you.”

Bernard smiled.

“Thank you.”

“Was it a record or a cassette?”

“A record.”

“I didn’t have a record player.”

Bernard stopped smiling.

Philippe thought about it.

“At least, I don’t think I did.”

“You did.”

“Possible.”

They talked for another twenty minutes.

As they were leaving, Philippe asked if Bernard wanted to redo his kitchen.

Bernard said he would think about it.

In his car, he took out the notebook.

Beside Philippe Moreau, he drew a small cross.

He looked at it.

Then he turned it into a tick.

It seemed less funereal.

* * *

Sophie refused to see him twice.

The third time, she agreed.

“But not at my place.”

“Of course.”

“And not for long.”

“Of course.”

“And don’t come with your personal-development nonsense.”

Bernard looked at the book lying on his coffee table.

“What nonsense?”

They met in a brasserie.

Sophie was fifty and wore her hair much shorter now.

Bernard thought she looked well.

He did not say so.

He had learned something.

“Well?” she asked.

Bernard had prepared his sentence.

“I wanted to ask your forgiveness for the way our marriage ended.”

Sophie picked up her glass of water.

“What way?”

The question surprised him.

“Well... you know.”

“No. Tell me.”

Bernard looked around.

“Nathalie.”

“Nathalie?”

“Yes.”

Sophie frowned.

Then she understood.

“Oh. Nathalie.”

Bernard felt the conversation taking a direction he had not planned.

“I know I hurt you.”

“With Nathalie?”

“Yes.”

Sophie put down her glass.

“Bernard, I asked for a divorce because you took money from our joint account to buy a motorbike.”

He looked at her.

“Not only because of that.”

“Mainly.”

“And Nathalie?”

“Nathalie was afterwards.”

“No.”

“Yes.”

Bernard calculated.

Sophie watched him calculate.

“You see,” she said.

“What?”

“Even with your apologies, you want to choose what I’m supposed to be angry with you for.”

He opened his mouth.

Then closed it.

The waiter arrived.

“Have you decided?”

Sophie ordered the dish of the day.

Bernard was no longer hungry.

He ordered the dish of the day too.

As they were leaving, he said:

“For the motorbike too, then.”

Sophie burst out laughing.

Bernard laughed a little.

“Sorry,” he said.

“Stop.”

“All right.”

In the car, he opened his notebook.

Beside Sophie, he put nothing.

* * *

Monsieur Girard was dead.

Bernard learned this from his son.

He had found the number in an old address book.

“Were you a friend of my father’s?”

“Not exactly.”

“A colleague?”

“I was his apprentice.”

There was a silence.

“Oh.”

Bernard had been nineteen when he worked in Monsieur Girard’s garage.

He had lasted four months.

In the fifth, he simply stopped turning up.

No letter.

No phone call.

He had merely decided he was no longer a mechanic.

“I wanted to apologise to him.”

Monsieur Girard’s son did not answer at once.

“For what?”

“I left from one day to the next.”

“When?”

“In 1994.”

Another silence.

“You’re calling to apologise for walking out of a job thirty-two years ago?”

Put like that, the project seemed less convincing.

“Yes.”

“My father had Alzheimer’s at the end.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Why?”

Bernard did not know.

The man sighed.

“Listen, I’m at work.”

“Of course.”

“Have a good day.”

“You too.”

He hung up.

Bernard remained with the phone against his ear.

Then he said:

“I’m sorry, Monsieur Girard.”

The kitchen did not answer.

He opened the notebook.

He began to draw a tick.

Then stopped halfway.

* * *

With Claire Dumas, it was different.

She remembered perfectly.

They had been twenty-seven.

Claire had worked with him at an insurance agency.

A position had opened up.

They had both applied.

Bernard had told the manager that Claire was trying to get pregnant.

He had no idea whether it was true.

Claire had not got the job.

Bernard had.

They met on a terrace.

“Why now?” she asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Are you ill?”

“No.”

“Are you dying?”

“Not as far as I know.”

“Then why?”

“I’m trying to put certain things in order.”

Claire smiled.

Not much.

“Your things?”

Bernard understood.

“Things.”

“No. Your things.”

She stirred her coffee.

“You know what’s funny?”

“No.”

“I was pregnant.”

Bernard looked up.

“Really?”

“Six weeks.”

He found nothing to say.

“I lost the baby two weeks later.”

“Claire...”

“It wasn’t because of you.”

“I wasn’t going to say that.”

“You were going to say exactly that.”

He fell silent.

“I hardly cared about the job. What hurt was spending years wondering how he knew.”

Bernard looked at his coffee.

“I’m sorry.”

“I know.”

“No. I mean it.”

Claire turned towards him.

“And what do you want me to do with that?”

He had never thought about the question.

“Nothing.”

“Then why are you giving it to me?”

Bernard did not answer.

Claire stood up.

“I’m not angry, Bernard.”

He looked up.

“It’s worse. I’d stopped thinking about it.”

She left.

Bernard remained alone in front of the two cups.

He did not take out the notebook.

* * *

He stopped for three weeks.

The book about forgiveness remained on the coffee table.

An electricity bill ended up on top of it.

Then his keys.

One evening, his daughter Emma came for dinner.

She was twenty-six and worked in a pharmacy.

She opened the fridge.

“You’ve got nothing.”

“There are eggs.”

“They’re out of date.”

“Since when?”

Emma looked at the carton.

“Dad, eggs don’t get better with age.”

“Some cheeses do.”

“They’re not cheeses.”

They ordered pizzas.

While they waited, Emma noticed the notebook.

“What’s that?”

Bernard picked it up before she could.

“Nothing.”

“Are you writing a novel?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“Why?”

“Because I’d have to read it.”

He put the notebook in the cutlery drawer.

“You’ve been strange lately,” Emma said.

“I’m taking stock.”

“Of what?”

“My life.”

“Oh.”

She opened a bottle of water.

“Let me know when you get to me.”

Bernard did not answer.

Emma took a sip.

“I was joking.”

“I know.”

The doorbell rang.

Bernard went to get the pizzas.

* * *

That evening, he checked.

Emma was not on the list.

He did not add her.

* * *

A few weeks later, he started again.

Éric forgave him before he had even finished.

“Honestly, I didn’t remember.”

“I did.”

“Then it was your problem.”

They laughed.

Nathalie, on the other hand, refused.

“No.”

“What do you mean, no?”

“I don’t accept your apology.”

Bernard looked taken aback.

“You have the right.”

“I know.”

“I only wanted to...”

“You just did.”

“All right.”

“All right.”

They remained there for another five minutes because they had both ordered something.

Neither wanted to leave before finishing.

* * *

After two months, the notebook was difficult to understand.

Ticks.

Names with nothing beside them.

One name crossed out.

Monsieur Girard’s half-tick.

And number ten still empty.

* * *

One Sunday, Emma asked him to help put up a wardrobe.

She had moved in with her boyfriend, Lucas.

Bernard did not like Lucas.

He had no particular reason.

That seemed sufficient.

The wardrobe came from a Swedish shop and contained one hundred and forty-seven pieces.

“They could have made it a hundred and fifty,” Bernard said. “For neatness.”

Lucas smiled.

“True.”

Bernard liked him even less.

They worked for two hours.

Emma came into the bedroom.

“Getting anywhere?”

“Yes,” Lucas said.

“No,” Bernard said.

One panel was upside down.

Emma sat on the bed.

“Dad.”

“What?”

“Do you remember my eighteenth birthday?”

Bernard kept unscrewing.

“Of course.”

“What did you give me?”

He stopped.

“A computer.”

“That was my nineteenth.”

“Oh.”

“You weren’t there for my eighteenth.”

Lucas stopped holding the panel.

Bernard looked at his daughter.

“Where was I?”

“Nice.”

The memory returned.

A seminar.

A meeting that ran too long.

Dinner with a client.

He had promised to be home before dinner.

He had called at twenty to eleven.

Emma had already blown out the candles.

“I called you.”

“Yes.”

“I thought I came back the next morning.”

“You came back on Monday.”

Bernard looked at the screw in his hand.

“Why are you asking me this now?”

“Because you seem to remember everyone lately.”

He looked up.

“What does that mean?”

“Nothing.”

“Yes, it does.”

Emma sighed.

“You’re apologising to people you haven’t seen in thirty years.”

Bernard went still.

“How do you know?”

“Your notebook was in the cutlery drawer.”

“What were you doing in that drawer?”

“Looking for cutlery.”

Lucas lowered his eyes towards the wardrobe.

“Can you give us two minutes?” Bernard asked.

“No,” Emma said.

“Why?”

“Because he lives here.”

Lucas gently rested the panel against the wall.

“I’ll make coffee.”

“Thanks,” Emma said.

He left.

“You read my notebook?”

“The first page.”

“It’s private.”

“Yes.”

“So?”

“So nothing.”

“Do you have something to reproach me for?”

“This isn’t an interrogation.”

“I’m asking a question.”

Emma looked at him.

“And if I say yes, are you going to get your pen out?”

The silence that followed lasted longer than she had intended.

“Dad...”

Bernard picked up his screwdriver.

“Finish it with Lucas.”

“Where are you going?”

“Home.”

“And the wardrobe?”

“You’ve got the instructions.”

He left.

* * *

In his car, he sat without starting the engine.

A family walked past. A child was crying. The father was carrying a box far too large for him.

Bernard looked at his phone.

Emma had written:

Sorry.

He typed:

It’s fine.

He deleted it.

Then:

Me too.

He deleted that as well.

* * *

That night, he opened the notebook.

He looked at number ten.

He wrote:

Emma.

The name fitted perfectly into the empty space.

That was precisely what bothered him.

He crossed out the number.

Not the name.

* * *

The next day, he wrote the names on a fresh page.

Philippe.

Sophie.

Monsieur Girard.

Claire.

Éric.

Nathalie.

The others.

Emma.

He looked at them.

Then closed the notebook.

* * *

For four months, Bernard apologised to no one.

He went to work.

Had his car repaired.

Called his sister two days late for her birthday.

Bought eggs.

He even threw away the book about forgiveness.

Then took it out of the bin because it had cost twenty-two euros.

He gave it to Emma.

“Why me?”

“I don’t need it anymore.”

She looked at the cover.

“Neither do I.”

“Give it to Lucas.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know. He reads.”

“So do you.”

“Not that.”

Emma put the book on the table.

They talked about something else.

* * *

In November, Sophie called.

“I found something.”

They met outside her building.

She handed him a box.

Inside were papers, a few photographs, an old charger and a small child’s watch.

Bernard recognised it.

“Emma’s?”

“Yes.”

The pink strap was broken.

Bernard turned the watch over.

On the back, he had had the words engraved:

Dad will always be on time.

He did not remember the engraving.

Sophie did.

“You gave it to her after school.”

“What school?”

She looked at him.

“You really don’t remember?”

Bernard felt he would have preferred to lie.

“No.”

“You were supposed to pick her up. She was eight. I was in Aix.”

Something came back.

A car park.

Rain.

His old phone.

“I forgot.”

“Yes.”

“How long?”

“Almost two hours.”

“Was she alone?”

“The headmistress stayed with her.”

Bernard looked at the watch.

“Why don’t I remember?”

Sophie gave a small, tired smile.

“Because you weren’t the one waiting.”

She handed him the box.

“Keep it.”

“Sophie.”

“What?”

He searched for his sentence.

“Nothing.”

“Good.”

She went inside.

* * *

Bernard put the watch on his table.

It stayed there for a week.

On Saturday, he changed the battery.

Nothing.

He took it to a watchmaker.

“It’s had water in it.”

“Can it be fixed?”

“Everything can be fixed.”

The man examined the mechanism.

“Well. Almost everything.”

The price was more than the watch was worth.

“Do it,” Bernard said.

* * *

Three weeks later, it was working.

It ran four minutes fast.

“I can set it,” the watchmaker said.

Bernard looked at the hands.

“Leave it.”

* * *

Emma was working that Saturday.

Bernard waited outside the pharmacy.

She came out at ten past seven.

“What are you doing here?”

“Waiting for you.”

“Why didn’t you come in?”

“You were working.”

They walked to her car.

Bernard had prepared several sentences.

None came out.

“You all right?” Emma asked.

“Yes.”

“You sure?”

“No.”

He took out the watch.

Emma looked at it.

“Where did you find that?”

“Your mother.”

She took it.

“Does it work?”

“Yes.”

She held it to her ear.

“It’s fast.”

“Four minutes.”

“You should’ve had it set.”

“I know.”

She smiled.

So did Bernard.

Then she saw his face.

“Don’t.”

“What?”

“Your speech.”

“I don’t have a speech.”

“Good.”

They stood beside the car.

“I remember the school,” Bernard said.

Emma looked up.

“Oh.”

“Well... now I do.”

“All right.”

“I left you waiting almost two hours.”

“One hour and forty-five minutes.”

“You remember exactly.”

“Yes.”

“I don’t.”

“I know.”

Bernard looked at the watch.

“For me, it was a day when I forgot something.”

Emma waited.

“For you, it was a day when someone forgot you.”

She did not answer.

Bernard felt the sentence coming.

He kept it.

“Is that all?” Emma asked.

“No.”

“Then?”

He looked at her car.

“How are you getting home?”

Emma stared at him.

“In that.”

“Yes. Stupid question.”

“A little.”

She opened the door.

Then stopped.

“Do you want to eat at ours?”

“Will Lucas be there?”

“Yes.”

Bernard hesitated.

“All right.”

“Be nice to him.”

“I am nice.”

Emma burst out laughing.

She got into the car.

Bernard was about to leave when she lowered the window.

“Dad.”

“Yes?”

“Can you come next Sunday for the wardrobe?”

“You still haven’t finished it?”

“We were waiting.”

“Why?”

Emma shrugged.

“We’re missing someone who knows how to do everything wrong.”

* * *

The following Sunday, Bernard arrived at two.

Emma opened the door.

“You’re early.”

“By how much?”

She looked at her phone.

“Four minutes.”

Bernard took the little pink watch from his pocket.

“It’s this.”

Emma smiled.

Lucas was in the bedroom.

The wardrobe was still unfinished.

Bernard put down his toolbox.

“You haven’t done anything?”

“We were afraid of making it worse,” Lucas said.

Bernard looked at the upside-down panel.

“For once, you were right.”

They dismantled almost the entire wardrobe.

By four, it looked less like a wardrobe than it had two weeks earlier.

Emma put her head round the door.

“Making progress?”

Bernard looked at Lucas.

Lucas looked at Bernard.

“Loads,” they said together.

Emma left.

They carried on.

* * *

That evening, Bernard took the notebook out of the drawer.

He found Philippe.

His tick.

Monsieur Girard and his half-tick.

Sophie, with nothing beside her name.

Claire.

Next to her name, he had written:

done

Bernard crossed out the word.

He turned a few pages.

Emma was still there.

Without a number.

He added nothing.

He closed the notebook.

* * *

The following Sunday, he arrived four minutes early.

The Sunday after that too.

On the third, Emma gave him a key.

“Why?”

“So I don’t have to keep coming downstairs to let you in.”

Bernard looked at the key.

“You’re sure?”

“No.”

He waited.

“Take it before I change my mind.”

Bernard put it on his key ring.

In December, the wardrobe was still standing.

It leaned slightly to the left.

No one suggested fixing it.
