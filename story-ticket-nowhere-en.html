The Ticket to Nowhere

Emil Margaryan

🇫🇷 Français🇬🇧 English

English literary translation of the French original

At twenty-three, Milan Kovář had watched far more people leave than he had ever met.

It was one of the drawbacks of working at the reception desk of a hotel three streets from Prague Central Station.

People arrived with suitcases that were too heavy and left with suitcases that always seemed lighter. Milan handed out keys, gave breakfast times, explained how to get to Charles Bridge, and repeated that no, the minibar was not free.

He knew the trains, too.

Prague–Berlin: a little over four hours.
Prague–Vienna: about the same.
Budapest: nearly seven hours.
Kraków: you had to watch the connections.

He had learned all of this for the guests.

He himself had never taken any of those trains.

His colleague Pavel found this worrying.

“You’ve worked in a hotel for four years and you never travel.”

“Surgeons don’t need to be operated on.”

“That has nothing to do with anything.”

“Which is why the argument is hard to refute.”

Pavel was thirty-seven, had two children, an ex-wife and a theory about almost everything. According to him, a man ought to have visited at least five countries before turning thirty; otherwise his character “started to harden.”

Milan had visited two.

The Czech Republic, where he had been born, and Slovakia, where his mother had taken him at the age of nine to attend the funeral of an aunt he did not know.

Pavel refused to count Slovakia.

“Why?”

“Because you were nine.”

“I was still abroad.”

“You had a Game Boy.”

“Borders don’t ban Game Boys.”

Pavel shook his head.

He often did that with Milan.

That Thursday, shortly before midnight, Milan bent down behind the desk to look for a pen a French guest had dropped.

He found the pen.

And an envelope.

It was white, with no name and no stamp.

Inside was a train ticket.

Praha hl. n. — Wien Hbf.

Departure: 8:44 a.m.

The next day.

With the ticket was a small piece of paper folded in half.

Milan opened it.

A single sentence:

If you found it, it’s for you.

Underneath, in smaller writing:

In Vienna: locker 214. Code 7316.

He read the two lines again.

Then looked around.

The lobby was empty.

A couple were asleep on the sofa near the entrance even though they had a room on the third floor. The coffee machine made the sound of laboured breathing.

Milan called Pavel.

“Did you leave something behind the desk?”

“My dignity, about three years ago.”

“An envelope.”

Silence.

“With money in it?”

“No.”

“Then it isn’t mine.”

Milan hung up.

He checked the ticket.

It was valid.

He put the envelope in the lost-property drawer, between a pair of glasses and a phone charger.

At 12:17 a.m., he took it out again.

At 12:22, he put it back.

At 12:31, he took it out a second time.

At 12:45, he went home with it.

• • •

Milan lived with his mother in a flat in Žižkov where the kitchen table was slightly too large for the kitchen.

His mother said it was not.

The wall said otherwise.

Whenever the refrigerator door was opened all the way, it hit a chair.

Milan came in at one twenty.

His mother was still awake.

“Are you working tomorrow?”

“Yes.”

“Then why are you making that face?”

“What face?”

“The one you make when you’re about to do something stupid and you’re still looking for an intelligent reason to do it.”

Milan put the envelope on the table.

His mother read the ticket, then the note.

“You’re not going.”

“I didn’t say I was.”

“You came to show me this at one in the morning.”

“That’s an observation, not evidence.”

She handed the envelope back to him.

“Who gave it to you?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then you’re not going.”

“Probably not.”

She opened the refrigerator.

The door knocked against the chair.

“This table is too big,” Milan said.

His mother turned around.

“Since when?”

“Always.”

“You see? That’s how journeys begin. You come home at one in the morning and suddenly your mother’s table is no longer good enough.”

He went to bed.

At seven ten, he was awake.

At seven twenty, he sent Pavel a message.

I’m sick.

The reply came almost at once.

With what?

Milan thought.

Stomach.

Three dots appeared.

You’re a terrible liar.

Milan switched off his phone.

At eight forty, he was standing on the platform.

• • •

He had imagined that getting on the train would make something happen.

Fear.

Excitement.

Perhaps even a revelation.

Nothing happened.

A woman merely asked him to move his bag because it was on her seat.

Milan apologised.

The train left Prague.

For the first twenty minutes, he watched the apartment blocks give way to suburbs, then fields.

He knew the names of the stations before they appeared on the signs. He had explained this route dozens of times to strangers.

It was strange to discover that the cities continued to exist after his explanations.

He checked his phone.

Seven messages from Pavel.

Two from his mother.

One from the hotel.

He answered none of them.

Beside him, a man of about sixty was reading a German newspaper.

“Are you going to Vienna?” Milan asked.

The man lowered his paper.

“That is generally what people on this train are doing.”

Milan decided not to speak again.

At 12:49 p.m., he got off at Wien Hauptbahnhof.

He did not go straight to locker 214.

He bought a coffee.

It was bad and expensive.

That reassured him. Some things remained stable from one country to the next.

He left the station, walked aimlessly for almost two hours, saw buildings whose names he did not know and refused to look them up on his phone.

Around three, he returned to the station.

His international experience now seemed sufficient.

He checked the departures for Prague.

Then, despite himself, looked towards the lockers.

Number 214 was at the far end of a metal row.

Milan keyed in 7316.

The door opened.

Inside: a white envelope.

For a few seconds, he did not take it.

Then he sighed.

“Of course.”

Inside were a ticket and another note.

Wien Hbf. — Ljubljana.

Departure: 4:58 p.m.

You could still have gone home.

Ljubljana: locker 86. Code 2041.

Something cold moved through Milan’s stomach.

He called Pavel.

“Very funny.”

“Thank you.”

“Was this you?”

“Was what me?”

“The tickets.”

“What tickets?”

“Prague. Vienna. Now Ljubljana.”

Silence.

Then:

“You’re in Vienna?”

Milan hung up.

Pavel called back.

He did not answer.

A message arrived.

YOU’RE IN VIENNA???

Then a second:

You told me you had diarrhoea.

Milan smiled despite himself.

He looked at the departures board.

Ljubljana.

4:58 p.m.

At 4:54, he boarded the train.

• • •

Ljubljana was smaller than he had imagined.

The problem was that he had never imagined it at all.

This time, before getting off, Milan checked his seat.

Nothing.

Under the seat.

Nothing.

In the seat pocket in front of him.

Nothing.

“Have you lost something?” the conductor asked.

“I don’t know yet.”

She looked at him.

“That sounds exhausting.”

He got off.

Locker 86 was only a few metres from the exit.

Milan walked past it.

He did not stop.

He took a room in a hostel near the river. Six beds. Five occupied.

A Spaniard called Álvaro asked him why he was in Ljubljana.

“I don’t know.”

“Very good.”

“Very good?”

“Me neither.”

“You don’t know why you’re here?”

“My girlfriend left me in Budapest.”

“And so you came to Slovenia?”

Álvaro shrugged.

“She went to Zagreb. I didn’t want her to think I was following her.”

Milan considered this logic.

“You could have gone back to Spain.”

“That would have made it look as if she’d won.”

Milan nodded.

For the first time since leaving Prague, someone seemed perfectly reasonable.

They drank two beers.

On the third, Álvaro asked:

“And you?”

Milan told him about the first ticket, then Vienna.

Álvaro listened seriously.

“Someone wants to kill you.”

“Thank you.”

“Or sleep with you.”

“Is that better?”

“Usually.”

“There’s a third envelope at the station.”

Álvaro raised his beer.

“Ah. Then you already know what you’re doing tomorrow.”

“No.”

“Of course.”

The next morning, Milan stood for a long time in front of locker 86.

He keyed in 2041.

The door opened.

Inside: an envelope.

Ljubljana — Trieste Centrale.

1:38 p.m.

On the paper, two words:

You looked.

Then:

Trieste: Hotel Miramare, reception.

Milan read the hotel name again.

He put the envelope back in the locker.

Closed the door.

Took three steps.

Came back.

Opened it again.

He took the ticket.

• • •

He called his mother from a bridge over the Ljubljanica.

“Is that you?”

“Hello to you too.”

“The tickets.”

“What tickets?”

“Mum.”

“Milan.”

“Did you know I was in Ljubljana?”

Silence.

“I knew you’d left.”

“How?”

“Pavel called me to ask whether your diarrhoea required hospitalisation.”

Milan closed his eyes.

“He’s an idiot.”

“Yes. But he worries very efficiently.”

“You didn’t arrange any of this?”

“With what money?”

The answer was maternal enough to be convincing.

“Why did you leave?” she asked.

Milan looked at the river.

“I don’t know.”

“Then come home when you do.”

“And if I never do?”

“You’ll run out of socks eventually.”

She hung up.

Milan remained on the bridge for a while.

He had always thought his mother would stop him from leaving.

She had just advised him to keep going.

He found that almost insulting.

At 1:38 p.m., he took the train to Trieste.

• • •

He saw the sea before he arrived.

Only briefly.

A gap between two buildings, then a blue line.

Milan sat up straighter.

He had seen the sea in photographs, obviously. On television. On tourists’ phones.

But he had never seen a city end like that.

In Trieste, he walked down to the water.

He stayed there for a long time.

He thought he ought to feel something immense.

Mostly, he felt hungry.

He bought a slice of pizza.

It was better than the Viennese coffee, which seemed to him a personal victory for Italy.

He did not go to the Hotel Miramare.

Instead, he entered a small guesthouse three streets from the harbour and asked for a room.

“One night?” the owner asked.

Milan looked at his bag.

“I think so.”

The woman looked up.

“You think so?”

“One night.”

He paid.

Then went out walking again.

At seven in the evening, he passed a hotel with MIRAMARE written above the door.

He stopped.

The glass door reflected the street behind him.

He kept walking.

Twenty metres later, he turned back.

At reception, a man looked up.

“Good evening.”

“Excuse me. Something may have been left for Milan Kovář.”

The receptionist glanced behind the desk.

“Yes.”

He produced an envelope.

“This was left for you.”

“By whom?”

“A woman.”

“What woman?”

The receptionist thought.

“A woman.”

“I gathered that.”

“Dark-haired, maybe.”

“How old?”

“Between thirty and sixty.”

“That’s quite a range.”

“I work at reception, not for the police.”

Milan knew that tone. He had used it himself hundreds of times.

“When did she come?”

“This afternoon.”

“Did she say anything?”

“Yes.”

“What?”

The receptionist thought again.

“She asked where the toilets were.”

Milan stared at him.

“That’s all?”

“They’re at the end on the left.”

Milan took the envelope.

He opened it in the street.

Trieste Centrale — Praha hl. n.

For the following day.

The note said:

Now you can choose.

There was no locker, no code, no other address.

For the first time, there was nothing after it.

• • •

He slept badly.

At three in the morning, he called Klára.

She answered after six rings.

“Has someone died?”

“No.”

“Then call back at an hour when people are alive.”

“Did you arrange a trip for me?”

Silence.

“What?”

He told her everything.

When he finished, Klára was quiet.

“That’s very romantic,” she said.

“So it was you?”

“Milan, when we were together, you refused to take the tram if I told you which one to take.”

“That’s not true.”

“Once you walked forty minutes in the rain to prove that tram 9 wasn’t necessary.”

He remembered.

“So, no?”

“No.”

“Do you know anyone in Trieste?”

“I know someone in Brno.”

“That doesn’t help me.”

“That’s also what I used to think during our relationship.”

She hung up.

Milan lay in the dark.

Then his phone vibrated.

A message from Klára:

You don’t know what’s waiting for you. Enjoy it.

He did not reply.

• • •

The next morning, he arrived at the station forty minutes early.

The train to Prague left at 9:27.

On another platform, a train to Venice left at 9:36.

Milan looked at the two boards.

Prague.

Venice.

He already knew the route to Prague.

He had a ticket in his pocket.

For Venice, he had nothing.

At 9:05, he called his mother.

“I think I’m coming home.”

“All right.”

He waited.

“That’s all?”

“What do you want me to do?”

“I don’t know.”

“Neither do I.”

Milan smiled.

“Mum?”

“Yes?”

“Why did you never ask me to move out?”

She was silent for a moment.

“Because I thought that if I asked you to, you’d stay just to spite me.”

“That’s possible.”

“I’ve known you a long time.”

The board announced boarding for Prague.

Milan stood up.

“I have to go.”

“Then go.”

“I’ll call you.”

“Buy some socks.”

She hung up.

Milan walked to the platform.

The train was there.

Doors open.

He took out the ticket.

A conductor was waiting.

Milan took three more steps.

Then stopped.

He looked at the ticket.

Prague.

He folded it.

The conductor looked at him.

“Are you getting on?”

“No.”

“Then why are you on the platform?”

Milan thought.

“Bad habit.”

He walked away.

• • •

The ticket to Venice cost him forty-seven euros.

Milan found this outrageous.

He bought it anyway.

On the train, no one had reserved a seat for him.

He sat by the window.

Before departure, he automatically looked under his seat.

Then in the seat pocket in front of him.

Nothing.

A small smile crossed his face.

The train set off.

At 11:43, Pavel sent him a message:

Where are you?

A few minutes later:

Your boss wants to kill you.

Then:

Your mother says you’re fine.

Then:

If you’re dead at least answer.

Milan wrote:

I’m going to Venice.

The reply came immediately.

Why?

Milan began to type:

Because…

He deleted it.

He tried a second sentence.

Deleted that too.

Finally he wrote:

I bought the ticket.

Pavel replied:

Yes but why Venice?

Milan placed his phone face down on the table.

Outside, the landscape carried on without waiting for his answer.

• • •

He returned to Prague five days later.

Under his own steam.

His mother was in the kitchen.

The refrigerator door knocked against the chair.

“So?” she asked.

“So what?”

“What was it like?”

Milan put down his bag.

“Expensive.”

“Europe?”

“Venice, mostly.”

She nodded as though she had always suspected as much.

Then she asked:

“Do you know who was sending the tickets?”

Milan opened the refrigerator.

“No.”

“Don’t you want to know?”

He took out a bottle of water.

“Yes.”

He closed the door.

It hit the chair again.

“But not today.”

His mother looked at him.

“You’ve changed.”

Milan sighed.

“I’ve been home for twenty minutes.”

“All right. I’ll check again tomorrow.”

• • •

The following Monday, he went back to work.

Pavel was waiting behind the desk.

“The man from Venice.”

“Hello.”

“You owe me two shifts.”

“Five.”

“I wanted to start gently.”

Milan put down his bag.

The lobby was exactly as before.

Same sofa.

Same sickly coffee machine.

Same smell of cleaning products and industrial croissants.

For an hour, he handed out keys.

At 10:15, an American woman asked him how to get to Budapest.

Milan explained.

At 10:40, a German couple asked which train they should take to Vienna.

He told them the platform.

“Do you know Vienna well?” the man asked.

Milan hesitated.

“A little.”

The couple thanked him and left.

Milan watched them pass through the automatic doors.

At eleven, Pavel went out for a cigarette.

Milan was alone.

He opened the lost-property drawer to look for an adapter.

Between the glasses and the phone charger, he saw a white envelope.

He froze.

Same paper.

Same size.

He picked it up.

This time, his name was written on it.

Milan.

He stood for a long time without opening it.

Then he smiled.

He put the envelope back in the drawer.

Pavel returned.

“What was that?”

“Nothing.”

“You’re not opening it?”

“Later.”

“You’re not curious?”

Milan looked at the hotel’s automatic doors.

Outside, someone was running towards the station with a suitcase.

Someone else had just come out of it.

“Yes,” he said.

He closed the drawer.

“But now I know I can leave without it.”
