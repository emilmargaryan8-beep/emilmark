Invisible Walls

Emil Margaryan

🇫🇷 Français🇬🇧 English🇷🇺 Русский

English literary translation of the French original

That summer in Rome, the heat seemed to have given up trying to enter the hospital.

Outside, the city burned beneath a white sky. Inside, the air smelled of disinfectant, cold coffee, and something harder to name: the odourless smell of places where people have been waiting too long.

Dr Lorenzo Anselmi closed the door behind him.

Mrs Calvi was already seated.

She always occupied the same armchair by the window, an old green velvet chair whose arms had been polished smooth by years of anxious hands. She sat very straight, her eyes fixed on the door, as though Anselmi had arrived late to a scene that had begun without him.

He rested his notebook on his knees.

— Good morning, Mrs Calvi. How are you feeling today?

She looked up at him.

— And you, Doctor?

He barely smiled.

— We’re here to listen to you.

— An elegant way of never answering a question.

Anselmi picked up his pen.

— You think I avoid your questions?

— I think you like deciding which questions deserve an answer.

He wrote down a few words.

She watched his hand move across the page.

— You see? Even now, you’re the one deciding what will remain of this conversation.

— Do you believe someone is trying to control what is said in this room?

Mrs Calvi gave a quiet laugh.

— Not someone. My husband.

The pen stopped.

— We’ve already spoken about your husband.

— That’s precisely the problem. Everyone talks about him. No one talks to him.

She turned slightly towards the window.

— He came yesterday.

Anselmi did not answer at once.

— Here?

— Here.

— You’re certain?

— He tried to kiss me.

Anselmi opened the file on the desk and scanned a page he almost knew by heart.

— Your husband has never visited you since you were admitted.

She smiled.

— Then your file is lying.

— Or you’re mistaken.

— Both are possible.

He looked up.

— You were admitted after several attempts to poison your husband.

For the first time, her smile vanished.

— Is that what he told you?

— It’s what the file says.

— Those are not the same thing.

A silence passed between them.

Anselmi knew this kind of conversation. Doubt was often the raw material of delusion: an error became evidence, a contradiction a conspiracy, a coincidence an intention. He had to stay with the facts.

— Why would your husband want to have you committed?

— Because a man who can no longer control his wife can always try to control the story told about her.

— And the medication found in his drink?

— You see? You don’t even ask whether it was mine.

He said nothing.

She leaned forward slightly.

— Don’t believe everything you read, Doctor. Files are like memories. Add a sentence, remove another, and one day nobody knows what actually happened.

— You think someone altered your file?

— I think you should ask yourself whether someone altered yours.

Anselmi smiled, wearily this time.

— I don’t have a file here.

— Everyone has one somewhere.

He lowered his eyes to write.

— Are your migraines any better?

The pen hung motionless above the paper.

Anselmi slowly raised his head.

— Excuse me?

— Your migraines.

She pointed to the base of her own skull.

— Here. They start at the back of your neck. Mostly at night.

He went still.

For three weeks a dull pain had been waking him almost every night. He had mentioned it neither to his colleagues nor to his wife. He had taken a few tablets from the medicine cabinet in the staff room and promised himself he would see someone if it continued.

— Who told you about that?

— No one.

— Have you seen me taking medication?

— No.

— Did a nurse tell you?

— You’re already looking for someone to blame.

— Answer the question.

She smiled again.

— That’s how it begins.

— How what begins?

— When the impossible becomes just plausible enough for you to keep trying to explain it.

He closed his notebook.

— The session is over.

Mrs Calvi did not object.

When he reached the door, she said:

— Tonight, when you wake up, don’t look behind you.

He turned.

— Why?

She shrugged.

— You see? You already want to know.

* * *

That night, Anselmi woke at 3:17.

The pain was there.

He lay motionless, eyes open in the dark.

Then he remembered her words.

Don’t look behind you.

He felt ridiculous.

He turned over.

There was nothing there.

His wife was asleep, facing the wall.

Anselmi closed his eyes.

He did not sleep again.

* * *

The next morning he arrived at the hospital before eight.

In his office, he reread the previous day’s notes.

“Marital persecution. Distrust of case file. Attempt to displace doubt onto therapist.”

Then, beneath it:

“Nocturnal migraines.”

He frowned.

He did not remember writing those two words.

Yet the handwriting was his.

He nearly tore out the page, then stopped himself.

At nine, Mrs Calvi came in.

She studied his face.

— You look tired.

— Sit down.

— Did you look behind you?

He ignored the question.

She sat.

— Mrs Calvi, how do you know about my migraines?

— I told you. I know certain things.

— What things?

— The things you’ve forgotten.

Irritation rose in him.

— Enough games. You claim your husband had you locked away here. You claim he manipulates your file. Now you claim to know details of my private life. Why?

— You always think there has to be a reason.

— Usually there is.

— Causes are comforting. They put events in a straight line.

— And what do you prefer?

— Circles.

She glanced at the notebook on his knees.

— What did you write about me yesterday?

— That doesn’t concern you.

— Curious. I speak, you write, and somehow it doesn’t concern me.

Anselmi opened the notebook.

The first lines were clear. Farther down, some of the words seemed fainter. He rubbed his thumb over the paper.

— Is something wrong? she asked.

— No.

He turned the page.

A single sentence stood there:

“I know things about you, Doctor.”

He froze.

— Did you write this?

She laughed.

— In your notebook?

— Did you touch it?

— You’ve been watching me since I came in.

He snapped the notebook shut.

— Were you ever treated in this hospital before this year?

Her smile altered, almost imperceptibly.

— Why do you ask?

— Answer me.

— You’ve finally started reading.

— Reading what?

— The walls.

He stared at her.

She stood before he could dismiss her.

— Until tomorrow, Doctor.

— The session isn’t over.

She stopped at the door.

— Yes, it is. This time you’re simply the one who didn’t hear it end.

* * *

That afternoon, Anselmi went down to the archives.

The room lay in the basement behind the laundry. Old case files filled metal shelves to the ceiling. A fan stirred warm, dusty air.

It took him twenty minutes to find CALVI.

Giulia Calvi.

Born 1948.

First admission: November 1974.

He stared at the date.

In 1974, he had been twenty-six, a junior doctor in this very hospital.

He opened the file.

Severe depression following the disappearance of her younger brother. Insomnia. Dissociative episodes. Transient persecutory ideas.

Consultant: Professor Bellini.

Then, on a sheet dated 19 November, a handwritten note:

“Patient claims that certain memories are being removed and replaced with others. Refuses to sleep. Says she sees a man sitting by the window.”

The pain began at the back of Anselmi’s neck.

The handwriting looked familiar.

Too familiar.

He held the page closer to the lamp.

There was no signature at the bottom.

He checked the next sheet.

The same hand.

And the next.

Certain letters had exactly the slant he had always disliked in his own handwriting.

He closed the file.

An archivist appeared in the doorway.

— Looking for something, Doctor?

— Who wrote the junior doctors’ notes in 1974?

She shrugged.

— The junior doctors, I imagine.

— They weren’t signed?

— Not always. It was a different time.

— Do you have the staff registers?

— Farther along, on the left.

He found his name.

ANSELMI, LORENZO.

Junior doctor, Bellini’s service.

November 1974.

He sat down.

He knew he had worked under Bellini. He remembered the professor, his thick spectacles, the smell of tobacco in his office. He remembered dozens of patients.

But not her.

Not Giulia Calvi.

He reopened the file.

On one page, a sentence had been underlined twice:

“The patient asks whether I am certain I am the doctor.”

Anselmi closed his eyes.

He heard her voice.

Not the voice of 1997.

A younger voice.

One he could not place.

— And you, Doctor, how are you feeling today?

He left the archives with the file under his arm.

* * *

The next day, Mrs Calvi was waiting for him.

— You were here before, Anselmi said.

She did not ask what he meant.

— Yes.

— In 1974.

— Yes.

— And so was I.

— Yes.

He placed the old file on the desk.

— Did I treat you?

She looked at the grey cover.

— You should know.

— I don’t remember you.

— Does that worry you?

— Answer me.

— You were young.

— Did I write these notes?

She considered the question.

— Some of them.

— Why aren’t they signed?

— Perhaps you didn’t want to sign them.

— Why not?

— Perhaps because they weren’t only about me.

His hands tightened.

— What does that mean?

She looked at him with a new softness, almost sadness.

— You were different then.

— Everyone was different twenty-three years ago.

— Not like that.

— Like what?

She touched a finger to her temple.

— You were afraid of certain things.

— What things?

— Of believing me.

He stood.

— That’s absurd.

— You see? Same words.

— What same words?

— The ones you used back then.

He came around the desk.

— My file says your husband has never visited you since you were admitted.

— Yes.

— And yet…

He stopped.

A memory had surfaced with startling clarity.

A man in the corridor.

Grey suit.

White hair.

A hand on Anselmi’s arm.

“Doctor, she’s dangerous. Whatever she tells you, don’t believe her.”

Anselmi touched his forehead.

Mrs Calvi watched him.

— And yet what?

— I’ve spoken to your husband.

— When?

He opened his mouth.

Nothing came.

— Several times, he murmured at last.

— And the file says he never came.

Anselmi looked at the old file, then the new one.

— He could have come without visiting you.

— Of course.

She smiled.

— See how easy it is to manufacture an explanation?

— He spoke to me about you.

— What did he say?

— That you were unstable. That you had tried to poison him. That he was afraid.

— And you believed him.

— I had objective evidence.

— What evidence?

Anselmi put his hand on the file.

She looked at it.

— The file.

He withdrew his hand.

Mrs Calvi stood.

— Those are your walls, Doctor.

— What walls?

— The ones you can’t see because you call them evidence.

* * *

Over the next few days, Anselmi slept less and less.

He checked Mrs Calvi’s medication, her admission reports, the visitors’ register, the observation charts.

The more he searched, the less the pieces held together.

According to the register, no Lorenzo Calvi had entered the hospital since his wife’s admission.

Yet Anselmi now remembered three conversations with him.

In the first, the man wore a grey suit.

In the second, a blue shirt.

In the third, Anselmi could no longer see his face.

He asked Teresa, one of the nurses, whether she knew the husband.

— I think I’ve seen him, she said.

— You think?

— A fairly tall man?

— Yes.

— With glasses?

Anselmi hesitated.

In his memory, the man wore none.

— Perhaps.

Teresa shrugged.

— There are a lot of families here, Doctor.

The answer stayed with him for the rest of the day.

That evening, he returned to the archives.

He compared his notes from 1997 with those from 1974.

The handwriting was almost identical.

On an old sheet he found a sentence he was certain he had spoken only three days earlier:

“We are here to shed light on what is real.”

Beneath it, in another hand, someone had written in pencil:

“Light can blind you too.”

Anselmi dropped the page.

The words were familiar.

Too familiar.

He could no longer tell whether Mrs Calvi had already said them to him, whether he had read them here before, or whether the sentence had simply been waiting in his mind for twenty-three years.

Pain flared at the back of his skull.

He sat on the floor between two rows of shelves and waited for it to pass.

* * *

The following Monday, he left his notebook on the desk.

Mrs Calvi noticed immediately.

— You’re not writing today?

— Who are you?

She looked disappointed.

— That is a very bad question for a psychiatrist.

— Who are you really?

— Giulia Calvi.

— And who am I?

This time she did not smile.

Anselmi felt something give way inside him.

— Did I hurt you in 1974?

She looked away.

For the first time since their sessions had begun, she seemed to search for words.

— You wanted to help me.

— That isn’t what I asked.

— You were convinced you had to bring me back to reality.

— And?

— You told me so many times that certain things had never happened that eventually I no longer knew which things had.

Anselmi sat down slowly.

— My brother?

— My brother disappeared. That was real.

— Your husband?

She did not answer.

— The poisoning attempts?

Silence.

— Me?

She looked at him.

— You, I don’t know any more.

That frightened him more than anything she had said before.

— Why did you come back here?

— I didn’t choose to come back.

— Your husband had you committed.

— That’s what I was told.

— You don’t believe it any more?

— I don’t know if I ever did.

He studied her.

— Then why talk to me as if you know everything?

Mrs Calvi gave a tired smile.

— Because you needed someone to know.

The ticking of the clock filled the room.

— My migraines, he said. How did you know about them?

She lowered her eyes to her hands.

— You had them then too.

— In 1974?

— Yes.

— I don’t remember that.

— I know.

— And the sentence in my notebook?

— Which sentence?

— “I know things about you, Doctor.”

She went slightly pale.

— I said that?

— You don’t remember?

She shook her head.

For the first time, Anselmi saw fear in her eyes.

Not the fear of a patient caught in a lie.

Fear exactly like his own.

They sat in silence.

Something had changed.

Until then, each had been able to believe the other was running the game.

Now neither of them seemed to know the rules.

* * *

Two days later, Anselmi did not come to work.

Nor the day after.

Teresa told Mrs Calvi he was ill.

On the third day, she said she didn’t know.

On the fourth, she answered:

— Which doctor?

Mrs Calvi laughed.

Teresa did not.

— Dr Anselmi.

The nurse frowned.

— Professor Bellini is your doctor, Mrs Calvi.

— Bellini is dead.

Teresa looked at her with the particular caution Mrs Calvi knew too well.

— Try to rest.

— How long have I been here?

— Mrs Calvi…

— How long?

Teresa left without answering.

That evening, Giulia stood at the window until Rome disappeared into the glass.

She thought of the green armchair.

The notebook.

The migraines.

1974.

1997.

She tried to put the years back in order.

They refused.

* * *

The next morning, the door opened.

A man entered with Teresa.

He had grey hair and a tired face, and held his hat against his chest.

Giulia stood.

— Lorenzo.

The man stopped.

— Giulia.

She looked at Teresa.

— You see? I told you he existed.

Teresa said nothing.

Lorenzo Calvi approached slowly.

— How are you?

Giulia stepped back.

— You never came.

— I come every week.

She felt the ground shift beneath her.

— No.

— Giulia…

— The register.

— What register?

She looked around for Anselmi.

The green armchair was empty.

There was no one behind the desk.

The notebook was gone.

She turned to Teresa.

— Where is the doctor?

— Which doctor?

— Anselmi.

The nurse looked at Lorenzo Calvi, then at Giulia.

— Mrs Calvi, sit down.

— Where is Dr Anselmi?

Teresa still did not answer.

Giulia rushed to the desk.

She opened the first drawer.

Empty.

The second.

Dressings, a thermometer, a few forms.

In the third, she found an old black notebook.

Her hands were trembling when she opened it.

On the first page:

“Giulia Calvi. November 1974.”

She turned the pages.

The notes were there.

The woman speaks of her missing brother.

The woman claims her husband alters her memories.

The woman says she knows about my migraines.

The woman asks whether I am certain I am the doctor.

Giulia stopped breathing.

— What is it? Lorenzo asked behind her.

She turned another page.

The handwriting changed little by little.

At first, firm and angular.

Then rounder.

More slanted.

On the final page, she recognised it.

It was her own.

A single sentence had been written there.

“I know things about you, Doctor.”

She let the notebook fall.

Her reflection trembled in the window.

For a moment she thought she saw a man behind her: pale suit, dark hair, notebook in hand.

She turned.

Only Lorenzo and Teresa were there.

— Giulia? her husband said.

She looked back at the glass.

The man was there.

Or his reflection.

Or a memory.

His hand rested against the back of his neck.

She did the same.

A dull pain pulsed at the base of her skull.

Giulia smiled.

— What is it? Lorenzo asked.

She was no longer looking at him.

In the glass, the man seemed to be waiting for her to speak.

So she whispered:

— And you, Doctor… how are you feeling today?

Teresa took a step towards her.

Giulia gently raised a hand.

Outside, Rome was dazzling.

Inside the room, no one moved.

There were no walls between them.

None that could be seen.
