Letters from No One

Emil Margaryan

🇫🇷 Français🇬🇧 English🇷🇺 Русский

English literary version of the French original

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday, between a telephone bill and an advertisement for furniture Gabriel Vernet would never have bought.

The envelope was white. His name in black ink. No return address.

He opened it in the hall.

“There's a night you've forgotten. I'm going to give it back to you.”

Gabriel turned the sheet over. Nothing. The postmark was Geneva, the day before.

His first instinct was the same as when he found a glaring error in a manuscript: look for what did not fit. The sentence was too sure of itself. It presumed an intimacy no one had with him.

At forty-one, Gabriel worked as a proofreader for a small publishing house near Plainpalais. He spent his days removing unnecessary words, spotting contradictions, asking authors how a character could be in Paris in chapter four if he did not leave Lyon until chapter five.

He liked things that could be repaired in pencil.

The letter offered nothing to correct.

He slipped it into a book and went to work.

Four days later, a second envelope was waiting for him.

This time there was no sentence.

17 November 1986

11:40 p.m.

Quai Wilson

Gabriel smiled. A date, a time, a place: at last the writer had made the mistake of giving him something he could check.

He took out his 1986 diary. On 17 November, in his own handwriting, were the words:

Dinner at Mum's. 8 p.m.

The memory presented itself at once. The flat in Carouge. The little blue-flowered plates. An overheated saucepan on a cork trivet. His mother asking why he never met anyone.

He could have stopped there.

But the next day at the office, while correcting proofs of a novel, something in a sentence irritated him: the character remembered a meal in absurd detail while claiming to have forgotten everything else.

Gabriel put down his pencil.

The blue plates.

Why the plates?

That evening he went through the papers of his mother, who had died two years earlier. In an envelope of postcards he found one sent from Lausanne on 18 November 1986.

I'm staying with Madeleine until Friday. Don't work too hard.

Gabriel read the card again.

Then the diary.

Then the card.

He did not think: my memory is false.

He thought: I have the wrong week.

The distinction mattered.

The next day at work he tried to settle back into his routine. He corrected three pages before realizing he had read the same sentence five times.

That night he slept badly. When he woke, the dinner in Carouge was still perfectly clear. It was the clarity itself that now bothered him.

The third letter arrived at the office.

Élise Morel.

Nothing else.

The name meant nothing to him.

After work he searched the newspaper archives. On 18 November 1986, a Geneva paper had devoted six lines to the death of Élise Morel, twenty-nine, found in the lake not far from Quai Wilson. The inquiry had concluded that she had fallen accidentally. She had left a restaurant alone shortly before half past eleven.

There was a photograph with the article.

Gabriel studied it for a long time.

Short hair. A mouth slightly crooked. Her gaze turned away from the camera.

He felt nothing he could name.

But before reading the caption, he knew the photograph had been taken in Annecy.

He pushed his chair back a little.

The caption read: Annecy, summer 1986.

He closed the newspaper.

The following day he went back.

He asked for the June, July and August 1986 issues. He found nothing about Élise Morel. There was no reason he should have.

Yet when he left the archives, he turned left instead of going home and walked to a street he had never intended to take.

He stopped outside a building in the Pâquis district.

Third floor.

Rear-facing windows.

He knew this before looking up.

An elderly woman was coming out. Gabriel held the door.

“Excuse me. Did an Élise Morel live here?”

The woman looked at him.

“Are you family?”

“No.”

“Then you're rather late.”

She moved to pass him.

Gabriel asked, “Third floor?”

The woman stopped.

“You know that perfectly well.”

He felt the blood leave his face.

“Do we know each other?”

“You don't know me.”

She looked at him for another second.

“But I've seen you before.”

The door closed between them.

Gabriel remained on the pavement.

He wished he could have felt ordinary fear, something that quickened the heart and forced a person to act. What he felt was colder: the sense that one part of him was always a sentence ahead of the other.

He walked home.

There he put a sheet of paper on the table and drew three columns.

WHAT I KNOW

WHAT I REMEMBER

WHAT I KNOW WITHOUT KNOWING WHY

In the first: his mother was in Lausanne on 17 November 1986.

In the second: he had eaten dinner with her that night.

In the third: Élise had lived on the third floor, facing the courtyard.

He sat before the page until midnight.

Then he crossed out the heading of the second column.

In its place he wrote:

WHAT MY MEMORY CLAIMS

That small correction frightened him more than anything else.

For a week, no letter came.

Gabriel kept working.

He corrected a cookbook, an essay on borders and two stories by a writer addicted to semicolons. He ate alone, slept badly, and stopped looking for the person behind the envelopes.

Instead, he began looking for his own errors.

In the publishing house archives, the falsified royalty statements stretched back several years. Always small amounts. Always dead authors, or heirs who rarely replied.

At first, the money had gone towards paying his mother's debts.

Then the debts had been cleared.

The withdrawals had continued.

Gabriel had no memory of stealing.

But he recognized his method.

That was almost worse.

The fourth letter contained only an address.

6 Rue du Stand.

He knew the building.

He knew it the instant he read the number.

This time he did not go there.

First he searched the newspapers for the address.

Marc Delcourt, a former layout artist at the publishing house, had died in February 1988 after falling down the stairs at 6 Rue du Stand.

Gabriel knew Marc.

The memory returned without resistance: tall, thin, too loud, forever putting a hand on someone's shoulder. They had gone for drinks together two or three times.

He even remembered something Marc had once said:

You correct everything, Vernet. One day you'll end up correcting your own life.

Gabriel did not know whether Marc had ever actually said it.

He wrote the sentence in the third column.

The next day he went to Rue du Stand.

The entrance had been renovated. He waited in front of the old four-digit keypad.

He did not want to guess.

His hand remained in his pocket.

A man came in behind him and entered the code.

3. 1. 0. 8.

Gabriel closed his eyes.

It was not the code that frightened him.

It was knowing it a second before the man's finger touched the first number.

He climbed to the fourth floor.

The landing was ordinary. Grey carpet. Two doors. A narrow window.

Gabriel put his hand on the banister.

A familiar tension ran through his palm.

And with it, nothing.

No image.

No voice.

Only a physical certainty: he had gripped that banister hard before.

He went back downstairs.

Outside, he had to brace himself against a tree for several seconds before he could move on.

The fifth letter arrived two days later.

Sophie Rey

June 1990

Gabriel did not consult the newspapers.

He went straight to Accounts.

An older employee, Madame Favre, had worked there for twenty-five years. Gabriel put the name to her.

“Sophie Rey.”

Madame Favre stopped writing.

“What about Sophie?”

“She worked here?”

“You know she did.”

Almost the same words he had heard outside Élise's building.

Gabriel felt an immense weariness.

“Tell me anyway.”

Madame Favre took off her glasses.

“She was with me for three years. She disappeared in 1990.”

“Was her body ever found?”

“No.”

“Did she know me?”

Madame Favre stared at him.

“Why are you asking me this today?”

“Because I don't know anymore.”

This time she did not smile.

“Yes. She knew you.”

“Well?”

“Well enough to be afraid of you towards the end.”

Gabriel did not move.

Madame Favre picked up her glasses but did not put them on.

“You came here after she disappeared. You asked the same questions.”

He looked up.

“When?”

“A few weeks later. You looked ill. You wanted to know what she'd told me.”

“And what had she told you?”

Madame Favre hesitated.

“That she'd found irregularities in the accounts. And that a dead man had noticed them before she did.”

Marc.

Gabriel did not need her to say the name.

He left the office.

On the stairs, for the first time, he understood that the letters might not be meant to teach him something.

Perhaps they were meant to stop him rewriting it.

That evening he laid the five envelopes on his table.

He compared the writing.

The first looked careful, almost schoolboyish. The second contained only numbers. The third, a name. The fourth, an address. The fifth, two typed lines.

None really resembled the one before.

And that, precisely, was what felt familiar.

At work, Gabriel often corrected the same writers over several years. Their handwriting changed. So did their vocabulary. But certain mistakes survived: a tendency to drop articles, the same kind of imperative, a preference for turns of phrase they believed neutral and which were not.

He reread the letters not as their recipient, but as a proofreader.

In the first: “I'm going to give it back to you.”

On the back of the fourth: “Don't go back there. Not yet.”

Under the flap of the fifth: “Don't ask where. Not yet.”

The same break. The same habit of isolating “Not yet.” after an instruction.

He took a pencil.

Almost automatically, he wrote in the margin:

same writer.

Then he went still.

He opened a drawer and took out an old manuscript he had proofread in 1991. His annotations ran across the pages.

In three places:

Don't repeat this here.

Not yet. Don't give the explanation too soon.

Don't return to this detail.

Gabriel laid the manuscript beside the letters.

He did not need an expert.

He needed to stop lying to himself about what he could see.

Under “same writer” he wrote:

me.

The word produced no shock.

It had the quiet finality of a correction that was right.

Gabriel did not sleep that night.

He searched for no hiding place in the flat. He returned neither to Quai Wilson nor Rue du Stand. He telephoned no one.

He reread everything he had established.

At four in the morning he took a fresh sheet of paper.

Not to leave himself a message.

To reconstruct.

Élise.

He wrote her name and waited.

Nothing.

Then he added the facts.

Summer 1986. Annecy. Third floor. Rear courtyard. Falsified accounts.

An image appeared.

Not Élise dead.

Élise alive.

She was sitting barefoot on the windowsill, accusing him of correcting the letters she wrote to him.

“Even when I love you, you find a mistake.”

Gabriel dropped the pencil.

There was nothing spectacular about the memory. That was precisely what made it difficult to dismiss.

Other fragments came, irregular and incomplete.

Eight months together.

Élise discovering the statements.

Élise telling him she would speak to the director.

The meeting on 17 November.

The car near the quay.

Her getting out.

Him following.

Then a break.

Gabriel stared at the blank space on the page.

He understood that for years he had taken that blankness for an absence.

Now he began to see it as something erased.

He wrote:

I followed her.

The rest came back out of order.

His hand on her arm.

Élise pulling away.

The parapet.

A sound that was not a scream.

Then the water.

He stood so abruptly that his chair fell over.

For several minutes he remained against the wall.

He wanted the memory to offer him another version.

It did not.

He sat down again.

Marc.

The name was easier.

Marc had seen his car near the quay. He had waited. Then he had asked for money.

February 1988.

The staircase.

An argument.

The banister beneath his left hand.

Marc stepping back.

Gabriel pushing him.

This time he could not call it an accident.

He wrote the whole sentence.

I pushed Marc Delcourt.

His hand began to shake only after he had put down the full stop.

Sophie was the hardest.

He knew the principle now: do not look for the image; establish the facts around the blank.

Sophie worked in Accounts.

She had found the irregularities.

Marc had spoken to her.

She had demanded an explanation.

She had disappeared.

Gabriel wrote:

I met her after work.

Then:

I told her I could explain.

Then nothing.

He waited.

At dawn a sentence returned.

Not an image.

A sentence.

“I'm going to see Madame Favre tomorrow morning.”

Sophie's voice.

He remembered his own answer:

“No.”

One word.

After that, the memory broke again.

Gabriel put down the pencil.

The pages on the table looked less like a confession than a set of marked-up proofs.

He looked at the crossings-out.

The additions.

The arrows.

The displaced words.

His whole life suddenly seemed like a text to which he had granted the one privilege he denied every other manuscript: the right to preserve its contradictions.

He picked up the last envelope.

He had not noticed it at first, but inside, beneath the flap, a sentence had been written in pencil.

When you get to Sophie, go to the personnel archives. File 90-R.

Gabriel waited for the publishing house to open.

At twenty past eight he was outside the archive room.

File 90-R contained nothing remotely dramatic: no confession, no compromising photograph, no object kept as evidence.

It was an administrative file concerning the disappearance of Sophie Rey.

Staff statements.

Timesheets.

Police questions.

Copies of correspondence.

Gabriel went through the pages.

Near the end was a sheet bearing his signature.

Statement of Gabriel Vernet, 21 June 1990.

He read it.

It stated that he had left the publishing house at 6:15 p.m. and gone straight home.

The sentence was unequivocal.

Beside it, in pencil, a correction had been written in the margin.

False.

Gabriel recognized his handwriting at once.

Below it:

You came back to this statement on 3 July 1990.

You reread it.

You wrote this word.

You asked for the file not to be destroyed.

Then one final instruction:

Exit register. 20 June.

Gabriel found the register.

On the evening Sophie disappeared, his car had left the company car park at 7:07 p.m.

Sophie’s departure had been logged at 7:03 p.m.

Gabriel sat looking at the two times.

Four minutes.

Not much.

Enough.

In the margin of the register, in his own handwriting, was an address on the outskirts of Geneva.

He recognized it.

This time he did not ask why.

He closed the file.

Madame Favre found him like that.

“Gabriel?”

He looked up.

“Did the police ask you where Sophie might be?”

“Yes.”

“And me?”

“You said you knew nothing.”

He nodded.

Madame Favre remained by the door.

“Do you know something now?”

Gabriel looked at the closed file.

He thought of the letters.

Of the precision with which someone had spaced them.

Of their almost professional pacing: never give the reader a truth before he can verify it for himself.

He thought of the sentence he had written on other people's manuscripts for twenty years:

Not yet. Don't give the explanation too soon.

At last he understood why none of the letters contained the whole confession.

He had not trusted the man he would be when he read them.

“Yes,” he said.

Madame Favre waited.

Gabriel tucked the file under his arm.

He went downstairs.

Outside, Geneva lay under the white light of late winter, which lent the facades a temporary look.

The police station was a twelve-minute walk away.

Gabriel knew the way.

He walked as far as the quay.

To his right, the city went on as though nothing had been discovered. Trams passed. A woman shook a tablecloth from a window. A delivery driver swore at a jammed wheel.

Ahead, at the end of the street, he could already see the police station.

He stopped.

In the file were his false statement, the register, the address.

In his head were Élise by the lake, Marc on the stairs, Sophie's voice saying she would see Madame Favre the next morning.

He did not know whether the rest would return.

For the first time, it no longer mattered.

He opened the file one last time.

On his 1990 statement, the word False was still there, written in his own hand.

Gabriel took out his pencil.

For several seconds he held it above the word.

One stroke would have been enough to blur it beyond recognition.

He put the pencil back in his pocket.

Then he started walking again.

At the next junction, the light turned green.

Gabriel crossed.
