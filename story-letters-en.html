Letters from No One

Emil Margaryan

🇫🇷 Français🇬🇧 English🇷🇺 Русский

English literary version of the French original

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday, wedged between a telephone bill and an advertisement for furniture Gabriel Vernet would never have bought.

The envelope was white. His name was written in black ink. No return address.

He opened it in the hall.

“There's a night you've forgotten. I'm going to give it back to you.”

Gabriel turned the sheet over. Nothing. The postmark was Geneva, the day before.

His first reaction was the one he had whenever he came across a glaring error in a manuscript: he looked for the flaw. The sentence was too certain of itself. It presumed an intimacy no one had with him.

At forty-one, Gabriel worked as a proofreader for a small publishing house near Plainpalais. He spent his days cutting unnecessary words, spotting contradictions, asking authors how a character could be in Paris in chapter four if he did not leave Lyon until chapter five.

He liked things that could be put right in pencil.

The letter offered no correction.

He slipped it into a book and went to work.

Four days passed without incident. Gabriel had almost stopped thinking about it when a second envelope appeared in his letterbox.

“17 November 1986. Quai Wilson. 11:40 p.m. Find out what you were doing there.”

This time he smiled. A date, a time, a place: at last the writer had made the mistake of becoming verifiable.

At home, Gabriel took out his 1986 diary. He kept his old appointment books in a sideboard drawer without sentiment, the way one keeps receipts that might conceivably be needed again.

On 17 November he found: Dinner at Mum's. 8 p.m.

At once he saw his mother's flat in Carouge. The little blue flowers on the plates. A saucepan set down too hot on a cork mat. His mother asking why he never met anyone.

The memory was so ordinary that it seemed beyond dispute.

The next day, however, something drew him back to the diary. His mother had had a sister in Lausanne, Madeleine, whom she visited every autumn. Gabriel went through a box of papers he had not opened since his mother's death.

He found a postcard sent from Lausanne on 18 November 1986.

“Staying with Madeleine until Friday. Don't work too hard.”

He read the two lines again. Then once more.

It was not the wrong date that unsettled him. People confused dates. It was the plates. He had seen them before he found the postcard. He could have described the light along their rims.

At the office he spent the afternoon correcting a mediocre detective novel whose murderer inexplicably became left-handed halfway through. At four, a colleague dropped a file on his desk.

“You left this in the archives.”

Gabriel looked up.

“I haven't been to the archives.”

She shrugged.

“Then someone's doing a very good impression of you.”

She was gone before he could ask what she meant.

That evening, in the pocket of his jacket, he found a parking receipt from Quai Wilson. It was dated the previous night, 11:46 p.m.

He stood beside his car for a while. He had no memory of driving anywhere.

He could have called the police. But what was he supposed to say? Someone was sending him unpleasant sentences, and he had found a ticket in his own pocket that proved nothing?

The third letter arrived at work, mixed in with the publisher's post.

“Élise Morel. Start with her.”

The name meant nothing to him.

After work he searched the newspaper archives. On 18 November 1986, a Geneva paper had devoted six lines to the death of Élise Morel, twenty-nine, found beside the Rhône. Accidental fall, the investigation had concluded. She had left a restaurant alone shortly before half past eleven.

There was a photograph. Short hair, a slightly crooked mouth, eyes turned away from the camera.

He felt nothing he could name.

Still, he photocopied the page.

Outside, he read it again beneath a streetlamp. The paper trembled slightly between his fingers. It was cold, and he preferred to blame the cold.

For the next few days no envelope came. The silence changed the nature of the affair. While the letters were arriving, Gabriel could hate their author. Without them, he caught himself waiting.

He stopped searching his flat. He set no traps. Instead he decided to live as though nothing had begun. He worked, bought bread, finished a collection of short stories, turned down dinner with colleagues. He wanted to deny the stranger the satisfaction of watching him obey.

Then one evening, as he opened the novel he was reading, a sheet of paper slipped from between the pages.

“Rue des Bains. Locker 18. You already have the key.”

He read the sentence again, then checked his pockets.

In a narrow compartment of his wallet was a flat key he had never noticed before.

He did not leave at once. He put the key on the table, poured himself a glass of water and studied it as though its shape alone might tell him how long he had been carrying it.

At ten o'clock he was on Rue des Bains.

Locker 18 stood at the back of an old storage depot. The key turned on the first try.

Inside was nothing but a shoebox tied with grey string.

He set it on a bench.

He had expected obscene photographs, money, perhaps a weapon. The idea of an elaborate setup still seemed easier to accept than anything else.

Inside: a silver earring, a man's watch stopped at 1:13, a brown button with a few threads still caught in its holes, three newspaper clippings and a yellowed envelope.

The clippings concerned three people.

Élise Morel, November 1986.

Marc Delcourt, February 1988, killed in a fall down the stairs of his building.

Sophie Rey, June 1990, missing after leaving work. Her body had never been found.

Gabriel had known Marc Delcourt.

The memory came easily: a tall, thin man who had spent a few months as a layout artist at the publishing house. They had gone for drinks two or three times. Marc spoke too loudly and had a habit of putting his hand on people's shoulders.

Gabriel did not know Sophie Rey.

At least, nothing came.

His attention returned to the three objects. An earring. A watch. A button. Ordinary enough to belong to anyone; unbearable once placed beside three deaths.

The yellowed envelope bore only two words: For Gabriel.

He did not open it.

He put it in his pocket and closed the locker.

On the way home he passed a police station. He slowed, then kept walking. The gesture shamed him, though the shame felt abstract, as if he were watching someone else commit an act of cowardice whose reason he did not yet understand.

At home he put the envelope on the table.

The telephone rang.

Gabriel flinched.

When he picked up, no one spoke. Only breathing, then the click of a disconnected line.

He stood there with the receiver in his hand.

For the first time, a simple explanation occurred to him: someone wanted him to think he was losing his mind.

The thought almost comforted him.

He thought of Marc. Of the old publishing house. Of authors humiliated by rejection letters. Of some slow, ridiculous revenge. He began making a mental list of people who might have access to his office, his post, perhaps even his flat.

No name on it convinced him.

At last he opened the yellowed envelope.

There was a single sheet inside.

“Not yet. You'd go looking for an explanation before you're ready to read the truth. Go first to 6 Rue du Stand, cellar 4. Then ask yourself why you know the code.”

The sheet slipped from his fingers.

6 Rue du Stand was the building where Marc Delcourt had died.

He did not go until two days later.

The building had been renovated, but the entrance still had an old four-digit keypad. Gabriel stopped in front of it.

He did not know the code.

Yet his hand rose.

3. 1. 0. 8.

The door opened.

He snatched his fingers back as though the metal had burned him.

There was nothing in cellar 4 that looked like evidence. Empty shelves, a bare bulb, dust. He was about to leave when he noticed a dark mark on the wall behind a pipe.

Someone had written in pencil: YOU'VE BEEN BACK HERE BEFORE.

Gabriel did not touch the words.

He left the building and walked without direction for a long time. What had opened inside him was still not a memory. It was worse: familiarity without an image. He knew the code the way one knows the way to one's own bathroom in the dark.

That evening he found Madame Keller, his neighbour, by the letterboxes. She had lived on the same landing for six years, and until then their longest conversation had been about a leaking pipe.

“Monsieur Vernet, you dropped this yesterday.”

She held out a black pen.

“That's not mine.”

“You had it in your hand when you came in.”

Gabriel stared at her.

“You saw me come home?”

“Of course.”

“What time?”

“Late. Two, perhaps.”

The night before, he had been at home. He was certain of it. He had been correcting proofs until midnight, the radio on, a pot of coffee going cold beside him.

“I was here.”

Madame Keller made a small, embarrassed gesture.

“Then I have the wrong night.”

She gave him the pen and went upstairs.

Gabriel entered his flat.

The photocopy of the article about Élise Morel lay face down on his desk.

On the back, in black ink, was a sentence.

“You keep looking for someone else. That's why you keep failing.”

His eyes dropped to the pen Madame Keller had just returned.

He did not compare the handwriting. He could not bring himself to.

From that evening on, he stopped trying to catch the writer of the letters. Watching a door or forcing himself to stay alert would get him nowhere. Whatever the mechanism was, it operated in a blind spot beyond the reach of his will.

Instead he did something simpler: on a sheet of paper, he wrote down only what he could prove.

Élise had died on 18 November 1986. His diary claimed he had been at his mother's the night before. His mother had been in Lausanne.

Marc had died in 1988. Gabriel knew the code to his building.

Sophie had disappeared in 1990. The box contained a button from a woman's jacket whose origin Gabriel could not explain.

Then he added: Someone knows my absences better than I do.

He lingered over the last sentence.

Someone.

Suddenly the word seemed far too convenient.

Two days later, the final envelope arrived.

It was neither in the letterbox nor on his desk. It was inside the lower compartment of the kitchen clock, behind the small wooden panel he opened every Sunday to check the mechanism.

Gabriel did not remember there being a compartment.

On the envelope: Open when you have stopped looking for the author.

He sat down at the table.

He thought of calling the police before reading it. He thought of dropping the envelope in the sink and striking a match. He even thought of walking out of the flat and never coming back.

Then he understood that all three were merely different ways of not knowing.

He opened it.

“Gabriel,

“if you're reading this, the other letters have done their job. By now you need to know two things: not all the memories you possess are true, and no one has been pursuing you.

“You wrote the letters.

“You began building this system in July 1990, after Sophie. Since then, whenever the truth has surfaced, you've left the man you would become a sequence to follow: dates, places, objects. You had already managed to move Élise out of your memory. Then Marc. After Sophie, you understood that forgetting was no longer an accident. It was how you went on living.

“So I'm going to write down what you've always refused to keep whole.

“Élise Morel was not a stranger. You lived together for eight months. She wanted to leave you. That isn't why you killed her. She had discovered that, at the publishing house, you were falsifying royalty statements for two dead authors and siphoning off small sums of money. You started because of your mother's debts. Then you carried on after the debts were gone.

“On 17 November 1986, Élise asked you to meet her near Quai Wilson. She had copies. She intended to tell your director the next morning. You argued in the car. She got out. You followed her to the edge of the Rhône. When she tried to leave, you grabbed her. She fell. You could have called for help. You didn't. You took the copies from her pocket and went home.

“The dinner at your mother's never happened. You built it afterwards. The flowered plates were real. So was the meal, but it belonged to another week. You didn't invent the pieces. You only moved them.

“Marc Delcourt saw your car near the quay that night. He said nothing for more than a year. Then he realised you had lied about where you'd been. He wanted money. In February 1988, you went to see him. You fought on the stairs. You pushed him. This time you knew exactly what might happen.

“Sophie Rey worked in accounts. In 1990 she found old irregularities in the royalty statements. Marc had spoken to her before he died. She asked you to meet her after work. You told her you could explain everything.

“You did not let her leave.

“I'm not going to write where you put her body here. The address is on the back of this sheet. If you still want a choice in what kind of man you are, take this letter to the police without going back there yourself.

“The earring was Élise's. The watch was Marc's. The button came from Sophie's jacket. You kept them not as trophies, but because after each death you began to doubt what you had done. You needed evidence against yourself. Then you hid the evidence and forgot where.

“This letter is not asking you to remember. It isn't even asking you to believe me. It gives you only enough facts to make it impossible to pretend that another man lived your life for you.

“There is no other Gabriel acting while you're absent from your own memory.

“There is no other Gabriel trying to save you, either.

“There is only you, at moments you later refuse to keep.

“You wrote this letter in your own hand. If the writing looks unfamiliar, look at the signature. You'll know it.

“And if, after reading this, you choose to forget again, know this much: you've already chosen the sentence that will start it all over.

“It begins exactly as the first one did.

“There's a night you've forgotten. I'm going to give it back to you.

“Gabriel Vernet 11 January 1993”

Gabriel remained still.

He had imagined a blackmailer, a witness, perhaps a man who knew him better than he knew himself. The letter had closed off every one of those exits.

He turned over the final sheet.

In the lower corner, almost lost in the margin, two words had been added in pencil:

“Fourth attempt.”

He went back to the words, then lifted the lid of the box again.

Beneath a false bottom he had taken for a reinforcing board lay three envelopes, pressed flat against one another. His name was on each of them. All three had been opened.

The first was dated July 1990. The second, April 1991. The third, October 1992. A number had been added to each afterwards: first, second, third.

He did not read them in full. A few lines were enough.

Élise. Marc. Sophie.

The three names returned. So did the objects. But the confession changed shape each time, as though its author had been searching, attempt after attempt, for a sentence strong enough to survive forgetting.

On the third envelope, in his own hand, was a note:

“If he stops knowing again, don't give him everything at once. Start from the beginning.”

Gabriel sat before the four letters for a long time.

What frightened him was not simply that he had forgotten.

It was the knowledge that he had already remembered three times.

That he had already lived through this moment.

He put the letters back in the box. For several days the police station remained only a few streets away, and he never crossed its threshold.

February moved on.

A few weeks later, Gabriel had settled back into the rhythm of the office. He corrected proofs, ate lunch at the same brasserie, returned the same greetings without thinking. Parts of February seemed oddly perforated, but that no longer troubled him very much. He had always had a poor memory for unimportant weeks.

The box remained in the cupboard.

He knew it belonged to him. He no longer knew why he avoided opening it.

One evening, while sorting the post, he noticed a blank sheet left on his desk.

He picked up his pen. The nib barely hesitated before writing:

“There's a night you've forgotten. I'm going to give it back to you.”

He looked at the sentence with mild irritation, as if he had copied something whose source he could no longer place.

In the lower corner of the page, he added:

“Fifth attempt.”
